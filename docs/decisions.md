# Decisions

Newest first. Each entry gives the decision, the reason and the alternatives considered. Re-open a decision only with a new reason, and record the change here.

## 2026-10-30: finite-element check of every load-carrying part; the structure is under-designed

`cad/strength.py` cuts sections along a load path and uses beam theory without torsion, so it covered 13 load paths and left out every motor ring, flange, crank and torso frame. `cad/fea.py` now checks them all: each link's metal parts (and the printed shells that carry load between them) meshed as one bonded solid, held at the face of the motor or bearing that carries the link, loaded through a rigid coupling at the next link's interfaces, with the strength check's load cases (a 3 × body-weight landing on one leg with 0.5 and 0.3 × body weight across it, a 0.5 × body-weight hand catch, a sideways fall onto the knee, each joint's peak torque). Each motor ring, flange, bearing bore and stub records the joint it serves and its contact patch (`Part.extra["ifaces"]`). Checked against beam theory in bending and thin-strip torsion (`tests/test_fea.py`), it reads 5-9% low at a 3 mm mesh, so its target is SF 2.2 on yield; stresses are smoothed along the surface over about a fillet's size, since the parts have no fillets yet. It takes about an hour, so it runs by hand after CAD changes, not in CI.

Result (worst case per part, SF on yield): the ankle cranks (9.7), ankle cross (34), foot (2.2 and up), torso base, flange and battery shelf (2.7-7), forearm flange (4.1), hip pitch flange (4.1) and neck bracket (15) pass. The rest fail, for two reasons the beam check could not see. **Torsion of thin plates:** the fore-aft part of the landing twists the hip roll bracket's front and stator plates (SF 0.2-0.4) and the shin plates (0.8-0.9); the hand catch twists the shoulder pitch bracket's plates (0.6). **Motor rings on three posts:** every rear ring carries its joint's whole load through three Ø8 mm posts (hip pitch ring 0.5, hip yaw ring 1.3, shoulder roll ring 0.7), and the pelvis carries one leg's landing into the torso through the waist motor's ring (pelvis back plate and gussets 0.1, waist and hip roll rings 0.2-0.4). Also near: thigh plate 1.3 (side fall), hip pitch box 1.2-1.3, torso shoulder plate and neck bridge 1.1. It also found CAD faults: the shin spacers are 2 mm short of the plates (sized with the 6 mm plate thickness, not the shin's 4 mm), the upper arm's printed shell does not touch its two rings, and the computer plate is attached to nothing; and the printed forearm shell reads SF 0.3 against the beam check's 7.2, which needs a closer look. So the parts need strengthening, not thinning, before they are made: closed sections or ribs where plates twist, and rings bolted straight to plates (or on a continuous wall) instead of posts, which also rules out the bought-standoff posts considered in the parts review. Alternatives considered: refine the beam check with torsion (still blind to rings and junctions); CalculiX through gmsh (gmsh could not rebuild several links' surfaces; fTetWild meshes any closed surface).

## 2026-10-29: the BOM gives the full cost to replicate; corrections after the 1.44 m redesign

The published cost was "about $12k" with the Jetson AGX Orin developer kit and the hand kits counted as owned at $0. Owned items now carry their current price, so the BOM total (about $17.5k at October 2026 prices) is what a replicator pays. The workbook ships with every status empty, so each builder marks what they own in their own copy. The biggest changes: the developer kit at $3,499 (NVIDIA raised it from $1,999 in July 2026); the STS3250 servos at about $97 each (was $45); online machining for the 12 parts too large for the desktop mill (about $1,200, not yet quoted); and battery-building, printing and hand tools, an NVMe drive and a USB hub, which were missing. The 7 CAN buses are covered by the Orin's 2 on-board ports, one dual-channel adapter per leg and one single-channel adapter for the battery. These fit the electronics bay's 3 adapter outlines; the CAN timing test confirms the choice (roadmap item 13). The Seeed actuator prices were confirmed on Seeed's own pages. Each item has one line with its full quantity and a spares column (one RS03 line: 11 + 1 spare), not one line per purchase phase; `docs/plan.md` says what each phase needs. The BOM is now one workbook, `bom/bom.xlsx`, replacing `bom.csv`, `sourcing.csv` and the CAD's two build-list CSVs. Builders can copy it and mark each line owned or ordered (empty means to buy), and its Summary totals follow by formula. `tools/bom/book.py` tidies it after edits, edits lines from the command line and checks it in CI. `cad.export` updates its two CAD sheets in place and keeps the builder's status columns. The training GPU stays outside the total as an optional cost. Alternatives considered: keep owned items at $0 (understates the cost for anyone else); CSVs as the source with a generated spreadsheet (two copies to keep in step, and the copy a builder edits is not the source). The cost of a workbook is that git diffs of it are not readable; `tools/bom/summary.py` prints the totals for review.

## 2026-10-29: shoulder roll below 0° with the arm forward; the tracker scores the head and the joints

The clap, the reach and the squat (arms forward) held the shoulder roll on its 0° stop in 24-87% of their frames: the hands could not come to the middle. Shoulder roll now goes to −10° with the arm 45-75° forward and to −20° at 90-135°, and stays at 0° with the arm hanging, where the upper arm meets the torso (`coupled_limits` in `description/robot.yaml`; `cad.covers --check` and `check_packaging.py` check every point of it). −30° was asked for and checked: there the shoulder yaw motor meets the chest shell at every forward angle and the roll link the torso frame at 60°, and past 135° the upper arm meets the head; it would need the shoulder rebuilt. Simulation and the retargeter take −20° as a plain limit and rely on the arms colliding with the body; the controller must enforce the coupled limit on the robot.

The motion tracker scored 14 bodies (BeyondMimic's choice, to weight the hands), which left the head out: it shook (46-60°/s RMS standing still, against 1-3°/s in the reference) and hung 17° off. Its 0.3 m body-position kernel also hardly noticed a crouch (99% of the reward for a body 3 cm off): it stood and walked 5-8° deeper in the hips, knees and ankles than the references, and with an arm overhead chose a different arm posture for the same hand (shoulder yaw 95° off in the two-hand wave). The head is scored again and a joint-angle term over the legs, waist and neck is added (weight 0.5, sigma 0.3 rad; `protomotions/envs/rewards/marvin_bot.py`, written by `setup_protomotions.py`). Over all joints it made the arm clips worse: the retargeted shoulder angles are ill-defined (the arms-raise reference twists the shoulder yaw by 175° for no change in the hand), so the arms are left to their body positions. The result: head turning 93°/s to 41°/s RMS averaged over the 25 clips, feet 5.4 to 4.8 cm and hands about the same (6.6 to 6.8 cm). The references also gained the head's motion (`add_head_motion.py`): the retargeter had held the neck at rest, so the look-around clip did not look around. Alternatives not yet tried: a style discriminator (AMP, as in NVIDIA's deployed G1 tracker), BeyondMimic's gain rule, a stronger smoothness term.

## 2026-10-29: a shoe-shaped foot plate

The foot plate and sole were rectangles. They now have a shoe's outline (`shoe()` in `cad/leg.py`): full width at the heel with rounded corners (the push-rod posts sit 40 mm either side), the widest point at the ball, and a toe that narrows in a slight V to a rounded point set towards the inside, like the big toe; the toe cap follows it. The narrower toe needed stiffening for a toe-first landing: the side ribs stop short of it (150 mm) and a 20 mm rib runs down the middle of the toe to near the tip (SF 3.3 at the worst station). Feet 1.108 kg for both (−67 g). The simulation keeps the rectangular contact box.

## 2026-10-29: a longer torso and legs; the battery high for a V-shaped torso; mass limit 42 kg

At 1.29 m the hands hung almost to the knees (fingertips 0.38 m, a person's at that height 0.49 m): the shoulders sat about 40 mm low against the chin, the upper arm hung 50 mm below the shoulder joint, and the bought hands are long for the size. The robot is now 1.438 m: the torso is 100 mm longer (shoulders, neck and head rise with it), the shoulder roll joint sits 25 mm below the shoulder pitch joint (was 50), and the legs are 50 mm longer (the thigh's 25 mm above the hip yaw motor, so the thigh plate still fits the desktop mill; the shin 25 mm, with the ankle motors kept where they sit relative to the ankle). Fingertips now reach 0.556 m against a person's 0.542 m, elbows 0.934 against 0.906, hips 0.48 of the height (a person's about 0.50). Inside the torso the battery, the widest part (140 mm), now sits high on a 4 mm pocketed shelf under the shoulder motors, the keep-alive pack low at the back, and the lower side frames lean in to the computer's width at the base, so the shell narrows to 160 mm at the waist (was 188): a slight V. The shin's side-plate straps widen from 38 to 44 mm for the longer shin (SF 2.3); the chest shell is printed in two pieces and the torso cover seams move up to 200 mm, so every printed piece still fits a 250 mm printer. Mass 40.41 kg bare and 41.38 kg with shells (+0.64 kg); the mass limit is now 42 kg with the shells (`tools/check_description.py`), the real gate being the knee's heating on stairs. Costs: the static 18 cm step check puts the knee at 118% of its continuous rating (was 109%) and the hip roll at 105% (was 103%); the walking, stair and motion-tracking policies were trained on the old model and must be retrained; the centre of mass is 78 mm higher. Alternatives considered (renders outside this repo): the shoulders raised 25-40 mm at the old height (the chin then touches the shell's collar looking down and turned), the torso 50 mm longer only, the legs 90 mm longer (hips at 0.50 of the height, but the knee at 127% of rated: it would need the parked 1.5:1 knee stage, kept as a future option), and a taller shell taper for a stronger V.

## 2026-10-28: an honest mass budget; a stronger thigh plate, thinner shin and pelvis plates

A weight study (finite elements and topology optimisation of the main plates, outside this repo) found three things. The mass budget was optimistic: the computer weighs 0.877 kg (the developer kit, weighed, without its power supply), not the 1.5 kg estimate, but the CAD has no fasteners and the budget no limb harness. Those are now items of their own (0.4 kg, about 4% of the frame, and 0.5 kg for about 7 m of harness), so the model reads 39.91 kg bare and 40.74 kg with shells, under the 41 kg limit, where an honest count before these changes was about 41.6 kg. Second, the thigh plate, a single plate beside the leg's line, is weak against a sideways fall onto the knee: at an estimated 1 x body weight across the knee, finite elements put the drilled 6 mm plate at SF 0.5. It is now 9 mm stock pocketed from the outside to a 2 mm floor (`lighten(..., floor=)`): SF 2.3 in that case by `cad/strength.py`, which gained the case, for +61 g a side (8 mm gave SF 1.8). Third, two plates had margin: the shin side plates go from 6 to 4 mm (SF 2.1, -0.26 kg for both legs) and the pelvis back plate from 8 to 6 mm (SF 2.1, -0.08 kg). Net: structure -0.22 kg, budget +0.28 kg. Open: the torso base, which a hand catch with the arm forward mostly twists (torsion is outside `cad/strength.py`; finite elements put it at SF 0.8), needs FEA and likely a pocketed, stiffer plate before it is made; the side-fall load is an estimate until falls are measured in simulation; a topology-optimised thigh outline could take back about 35 g of the 61. Not taken: thinner hip pitch box walls (torsion unchecked), single-sided joints (needs the RS04 output bearing's moment rating) and a 13S2P battery (runtime at the 1 h limit).

## 2026-10-28: wider shoulder yaw, hip roll, hip yaw and waist yaw ranges

Motion tracking showed narrow ranges limiting skills: the retargeted two-hand wave and reach held the shoulder yaw on its 90° stop, and the trackers' sidesteps drove the hip roll to its −20° stop. Four ranges widen with no part, mass or cost change: shoulder yaw ±150° (was ±90°), hip roll −30° inward (was −20°), hip yaw ±90° (was ±45°) and waist yaw ±150° (was ±90°). `cad.covers --check` finds no shell contacts and `check_packaging.py` no errors within a limb; the new contacts are between limbs (one leg against the other at hip roll −30° with both straight, a forearm against the pelvis at waist yaw beyond ±70° with the arms down), which the robot must avoid by control, as it already avoided the same contacts at the old limits. The harness service loops at the yaw joints grow with the range (`electrical/harness.yaml`); their flex life at ±150° is unchecked. Re-retargeted, the two-hand wave and reach now use 93-103° of shoulder yaw; the other clips did not need the extra room. The walking and stair policies were trained with the old limits and are unaffected until retrained. Not changed: shoulder roll below 0°, which the clap needs, puts the upper arm into the torso with the arm at the side and needs a limit that depends on shoulder pitch, in the checks and on the robot. Alternatives considered: waist roll and pitch joints (about +2.5 kg and +$630; to be tried in simulation first), and a 1.5:1 knee stage with a 150° knee (parked).

## 2026-10-28: Kimodo motions with root paths; trackers step instead of hopping

The trackers now learn from motions generated with Kimodo (NVIDIA's text-to-motion model) rather than 100STYLE: they tracked as well once the prompts asked for a pace Marvin Bot can keep, and Kimodo can make the skills beyond walking (gestures, squats, a kick, a dance) that no openly licensed motion capture has. `sim/motion/generate_kimodo.py` gives each locomotion clip and in-place skill a dense root path, so the speed and turn rate are set rather than worded, and keeps the best of four samples by foot sliding and crossing; the clips go through ProtoMotions' own Kimodo route (its SOMA converter and keypoint extraction) instead of a converter of ours. Three fixes made the trackers walk properly. The reference walks floated 3-5 cm: the sole fix lifted each clip until its lowest foot corner touched the floor, and that corner is a toe tipped down at push-off, so the stance foot hung in the air; it now puts the flatter foot on the floor. The trackers hopped even on grounded references, both feet down 70% of the time and both up 20% (the reference: 10% and 0-8%), since BeyondMimic's rewards compare body positions only; a penalty on each foot whose contact differs from the reference's fixes it at -2.0 (both feet up 1-2%), where -0.5 only halved it and a tighter body-position tolerance (sigma 0.15) did less. And the palms faced forward because the stance leaves the wrist yaw at 0; the motion pipeline's pose turns them in (80°) and the retargeter holds them there. On eleven locomotion clips the tracker follows nine after 2,600 epochs (82-101% of the reference's distance, bodies within 3-5 cm), including walking backwards; it falls on the sidesteps, where Kimodo tends to cross or close the feet. Alternatives considered: 100STYLE (kept as an option; locomotion only), and recording our own motion capture (later, for skills Kimodo does not know).

## 2026-10-27: 100STYLE for motion tracking; the tracker's anchor-position reward

The motion trackers learn from 100STYLE (CC-BY-4.0) instead of ProtoMotions' sample motions, which come from BONES-SEED, a dataset licensed only to non-profit academic institutions and companies under $1M annual revenue (not to individuals, and not compatible with an open project anyone may reuse). `sim/motion/convert_100style.py` turns its BVH files into the retargeter's keypoints in ProtoMotions' SOMA convention, so the rest of the pipeline is unchanged. The same eight motions from three openly licensed sources were retargeted and tracked the same way (1,400 epochs): 100STYLE kept the tracker up on 5 of 8 clips, Kimodo's text-to-motion (SOMA-RP model) on 3 of 8 (5 of 8 once the prompts asked for a slower pace), and CMU's database on 3 of the 5 that passed ProtoMotions' motion filter (its feet also slid six times as much). Kimodo stays the way to make motions no dataset has, though its text encoder brings in Llama 3's licence. Trackers 1-4 stood still because ProtoMotions' BeyondMimic config leaves out the global anchor-position reward, so nothing paid for keeping up; `setup_protomotions.py` now writes the tracker experiment from upstream's with that reward, a fell-behind termination, the reference torso's offset in the observations, softer gains (action scale at least 0.4 rad) and more exploration. Alternatives considered: BONES-SEED under its licence (rules out most users), AMASS and LAFAN1 (non-commercial or no-derivatives licences), and mjlab's BeyondMimic task (kept as a fallback).

## 2026-10-27: bought four-finger hands on a wrist pitch joint

Two open-source 8-DOF four-finger hand kits (bought from Seeed Studio, SKUs 100062181 and 100063642; eight Feetech SCS0009 servos each at 5 V, 0.40 kg as published, mechanical design CC-BY-4.0) replace the 1-DOF grippers. The gripper's STS3250 becomes a wrist pitch joint (±60°) under the RS00 wrist yaw, so the hand both turns and tilts: the servo hangs from the RS00's output in a printed cradle, its case running up from its shaft, and a printed U on its horn and an idler pin carries a plate for the hand's wrist interface (`cad/arm.py`). The hands are the AmazingHand (Pollen Robotics): its CC-BY-4.0 design lets the real hand meshes ship in `description/third_party/amazing_hand/` with attribution, under their own licence, and they are used for the visuals and the joint sweep at the published mass; the mount plate carries the hand's six M3 holes on a 42 mm circle. Holding the hand level takes about 0.4 N·m (25% of the servo's rated torque), and with 0.5 kg at the fingertips about 1.2 N·m (75% of rated, 24% of stall): enough for pick-and-place, not for holding a load out for minutes. Alternatives considered: an RS00 for pitch (overkill, +0.24 kg at the end of the arm), an STS3215 (2.9 N·m stall, too little margin), and keeping the gripper with compliant fingers (cheaper, but the hands were already bought).

Proportions: with the pitch servo's case above its shaft, the wrist pitch axis sits 0.063 m under the wrist yaw (the gripper's pivot was 0.090), so the forearm is 0.188 m to the wrist axis, a person's at Marvin Bot's height (0.146 of height). The shoulder-to-fingertip length is 0.62 m, about 9% longer than a person's at this height, all of it from the hand, which is about 30% larger than a person's scaled down; nothing smaller is on hand.

Mass: the hands and mounts add 0.73 kg (grippers 0.19 kg out, hands 0.80 kg and mounts 0.12 kg in): 39.86 kg bare, 40.69 kg with the body shells. The hard limit now applies to the robot as it would run, hands and shells included, and is set to 41 kg (`tools/check_description.py`). The hands' servos need a 12 V to 5 V converter at each wrist and a serial bus of their own (SCS protocol; the other servos use STS), one USB adapter per hand. The joint sweep counts the palm with the arm. The walking and stair policies were trained without the hands and are to be retrained; the motion pipeline's arm scale is now the reach to the wrist pitch axis (0.79 of the source's).

## 2026-10-27: no side-to-side waddle; command and sensor delays

Flat v6 and rough v8 tipped their whole body over each stance foot: pelvis and torso roll 20-22° peak to peak (a person walks with about 5-8°), which swung the head 16.5 cm side to side while the pelvis itself moved under 2 cm. The waist only turns, so the torso rolls with the pelvis; the only roll penalty was the general flat-orientation term (0.02 at 8°); the air-time reward (2.0) paid for time on one foot, so the gait had no double support (0-2% of the stride) and slow 0.6-0.75 s swings; and the feet landed 21-24 cm apart. mjlab's G1 velocity task handles the same problem with an upright term, tight hip and ankle roll while walking and no single-support reward. The walking tasks now penalize pelvis roll (−10 on its squared sine) and a step width more than 2 cm from 16 cm, hold the hip and ankle roll near stance (−0.3, was −0.1 on hip roll and yaw), and pay air time at 0.5. The pelvis-height allowance is 2 cm (was 4). Commands also reach the motors 0-10 ms late and the IMU and joint readings reach the policy 0-20 ms late (random per episode), which Isaac Lab's DC motor model has no option for: `robot_cfg.py`'s `DelayedDCMotor` adds it, and since Newton's own actuator kernels only take a fixed delay on a plain PD motor, the motors run in Isaac Lab (about 2.5× slower per iteration). Flat v7 and rough v9 (resumed from v6 and v8) roll 4-6°, swing the head 3-8 cm, step 14-15 cm wide and pass every check; rough v9 is as reliable on 15-20 cm stairs over five seeds as v8 (2 falls in 15 runs each). `check_policy.py` prints head and pelvis sway, roll and step width. Alternative considered: moving training to mjlab, whose G1 task suggested the fix; not needed, since Isaac Lab 3.0 on Newton already runs the same physics.

## 2026-10-27: motion-tracking pipeline (in progress)

For skills beyond walking, motion tracking (BeyondMimic) scales better than reward design: a new skill is a new clip. `sim/motion/` adds Marvin Bot to NVIDIA's ProtoMotions (generated model, retargeting URDF and robot config) and retargets human motion with its PyRoki retargeter, overriding the link map and body scale. Eight walking, jogging and turning clips from ProtoMotions' samples retarget within every joint limit, with 7-14° of pelvis roll; ProtoMotions' height fix assumes G1's foot frame, so `fix_sole_height.py` puts the soles on the floor. The first tracker (5,800 epochs on Newton) counted all eight clips as tracked, but ProtoMotions' test only checks torso height: it shuffled on the spot, covering 0-32% of the reference's distance with its knees at about half the reference's bend. Two model problems were behind it: the gripper boxes overlap the thighs even with the arms hanging, so the arms held the legs (the arms now collide with the ground only; the retargeted arms still pass through the body and need a collision-aware retarget before hardware), and the scaled human feet were 12-14 cm apart for 11 cm wide feet (knees and feet now move out 1.5 and 3 cm). `check_tracking.py` compares distance, joints and body positions with the reference. A second tracker is training. The command-driven walking and stair policies stay: a tracker needs a reference motion to follow. Motion data is not committed.

## 2026-10-26: straighter stance; level feet in swing

The default stance's knee goes from 25° to 15° (hip pitch 12° to 6°, ankle pitch 13° to 9°, which keeps the foot flat and the CoM 106 mm inside the feet; pelvis 0.636 to 0.645 m), so the robot stands straighter and the pelvis-height term aims higher. The policies also swung their feet toe down by up to 30° (the heel high), because the foot-level term only covered feet in contact; a matching term now penalizes the tilt of feet in the air, and the MuJoCo check prints swing pitch and touchdown pitch. Flat v6 and rough v8 (resumed from v5 and v7) swing their feet at most 11-16° toe down and pass every check. The stair check now measures the climb from the walking height on the approach rather than the standing height, which counted the dip of walking against the climb (it failed rough v8 on 5 cm stairs it crossed). Not done: the rough policy still walks 3.8 cm under its stance height, inside the term's 4 cm allowance; a tighter allowance is the next lever.

## 2026-10-26: posture terms in the walking rewards

The stair policy (rough v6) climbed 15 cm stairs in a deep crouch: it stood with its knees at 95° and its pelvis 19 cm below the stance height, and climbed 8 cm low, which also ran its knees at 73-81% of their rated torque. Nothing in the rewards asked for height, and a crouch is stable. Two terms were added to both walking tasks: a linear penalty on the pelvis sitting more than 4 cm below its stance height above the lower foot (measured from the lower foot so it holds on stairs), and an L1 penalty on the leg joints' distance from the stance pose while commanded to stand. The rough task also doubled the heat penalty and put 14-20 cm stairs on a tenth of the tiles on every curriculum row. Resumed from v6 for 3000 iterations, rough v7 stands in the stance pose, climbs 4 cm taller, and passes 5-20 cm stairs (v6 fell on 18 and 20 cm). The posture check is now printed by `sim/mujoco/check_policy.py`. Alternatives considered: a straighter default stance (fewer degrees at the knee), which changes the standing check and every policy, left as an option; a base-height target from the terrain map, rejected because the map averages the stairs ahead.

## 2026-10-26: electronics bay, battery cradle and limb-root bulkheads

The bought electronics were one estimate box each in the torso and pelvis. They are now laid out at typical outlines for their class (`cad/electrical.py`), placed so nothing overlaps the frame, the motors or each other, and in the order the power flows: the battery slides out of the back of the torso between printed side guides and a latch plate, and its blind-mate connector seats in a socket over the computer, beside its buttons; the bay above the computer holds the contactor and precharge, the torso power board, the e-stop relay, the computer's DC-DC (fed ahead of the contactor) and the three USB-CAN adapters beside the computer's USB ports; the pelvis channel under the waist motor holds the 12 V servo DC-DC and the IMU, and the leg power board sits between the hip roll motors. A 150 W chassis-mount 12 V module does not fit the channel, so the BOM now asks for a compact 10 A brick (60 × 34 × 25 mm at most). Each limb's harness ends at a bulkhead on its parent module (legs under the pelvis board, arms on the shoulder plates) with one power and one signal connector, so a limb unplugs as a unit; the generated harness names the bulkhead and a clip pair (parent link, child link) for every service loop, and its leg chain now follows the roll-first order. The chest shell went to 2 mm and the connector masses moved from the harness allowance to the bulkheads to stay inside the limit: 39.13 kg bare, 39.96 kg with shells. Alternative considered: a single electronics box on the back; rejected because it would sit outside the battery's slide-out path and lengthen every harness.

## 2026-10-26: real actuator outlines and mounts

The actuators were modelled as plain cylinders at their overall size. RobStride's outline drawings (manual §1.1) and STEP models show each is a full-diameter body with the output on its front face and a smaller driver cap (with the cable exit) proud of its rear face: RS04 Ø120 body, Ø94 × 13.2 mm cap; RS03 Ø98 (ears to ~Ø107), ~Ø78 × 12.5 mm cap; RS06 Ø85, Ø70 × 14 mm cap; RS00 a plain Ø57 cylinder. Their stator hole circles are larger than the rings drawn before (RS04 10 × M4 on Ø106, RS03 8 × M4 on Ø98 in the ears, RS06 8 × M3 on Ø82). Each motor's output direction is now in `robot.yaml`, and its stepped outline is used in the URDF visuals, the packaging check and the part sweep. Every mount was reworked to bolt where the motor really bolts (`cad/mounts.py`): a 4 mm ring on the body's rear face with the stator holes and a hole for the cap; where a plate carries a far-side bearing stub over the cap (hip roll, hip pitch, knee, ankles) or must stay solid for strength (shoulder roll), posts join the ring to that plate, which became a light three-spoke spider where it no longer carries bolts; output flanges carry the output hole circle and a 1 mm relief outside the rotating flange. ## 2026-10-25: six motor CAN buses plus one battery bus

Robstride uses classic CAN 2.0B with 29-bit identifiers at 1 Mbit/s, and each motor needs a command and a reply frame per cycle. Six motors on one bus at 500 Hz would load it to roughly 80–90%. Each leg gets two buses (hip: 3 motors; shank: knee and two ankle motors) at 500 Hz; each arm gets one bus at 250 Hz, with the waist on the right arm's bus. All stay at or below 48% load (worst-case 160-bit frames). A seventh bus carries battery telemetry. The Orin's two on-board CAN controllers (with 3.3 V transceivers) and isolated USB-CAN adapters (gs_usb, SocketCAN) provide the buses; a microcontroller bridge is the fallback if bench tests show too much jitter.

## 2026-10-25: power: 13S3P quick-release pack, keep-alive supply, 4.1 V charging

A 13S3P pack of 21700 high-discharge cells (~630 Wh, ~3.4 kg) gives about 1.5× the runtime of 13S2P for ~1.1 kg more. It sits on a quick-release cradle with an anti-spark blind-mate connector. A small keep-alive battery feeds only the computer through an ideal-diode OR, so the computer stays up during a swap. A full 13S pack is 54.6 V against the actuators' 60 V limit, so regen spikes are a real risk: charge to 4.1 V per cell (53.3 V) for daily use and fit a clamp at about 56–58 V. DC-DC converters are rated for 75 V input, and the computer's supply is wired upstream of the motor contactor so an e-stop never reboots it. Main path: Class-T fuse, precharge, contactor.
Alternatives: 13S2P (lighter, ~1 h); two packs OR-ed for swapping while walking (v2).

## 2026-10-25: sensing: active-stereo depth camera, low-latency IMU

Head camera: Orbbec Gemini 335L. It computes depth on the camera, leaving the Orin's GPU free; its IR pattern gives depth on plain floors and carpeted stairs; it connects over USB-C; and Isaac Sim 6.2 has a built-in model of it. Driver support on JetPack 7.2 must be confirmed before ordering.
IMU: the locomotion policy needs a low-latency gyro at 500 Hz or more. A low-cost USB AHRS module tops out around 200 Hz, so it is the backup; the primary will be an industrial AHRS or a BMI088 read by a microcontroller, decided after a bench test.
Alternatives: ZED X Mini (needs a capture card and computes depth on the GPU), RealSense D455 (USB stability reports on Jetson).

## 2026-10-25: compute and software stack

Onboard: Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB developer kit (already available), JetPack 7.2, Isaac ROS 4.6 and ROS 2 Jazzy. Isaac ROS 5.0 is skipped for now because of reported regressions on Orin. A real-time control process (CAN I/O, 50 Hz policy, safety) runs outside ROS. Training: Isaac Lab 3.0 with rsl_rl, MuJoCo as a cross-check. Manipulation: GR00T N1.7, served from a desktop GPU first and moved to the Orin with TensorRT later. Whole-body control starts with the RL lower body plus IK arms; a motion-tracking controller (retargeting with GMR, BeyondMimic-style training) comes later. NVIDIA's SONIC checkpoints are for another robot and need far more compute to retrain, so it is deferred.
Alternatives: Jetson Thor T4000 (same module price in 2026; worth considering for a second robot).

## 2026-10-25: vendor CAD is linked, not committed

Robstride's STEP models are public but carry no licence, and several CAD sites forbid redistribution or derived designs. The repo will fetch vendor models into a git-ignored folder with a script and use its own simplified envelopes in committed designs. Fasteners and bearings come from parametric libraries with open licences (bd_warehouse, Apache-2.0).
