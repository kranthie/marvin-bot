# Decisions

Newest first. Each entry gives the decision, the reason and the alternatives considered. Re-open a decision only with a new reason, and record the change here.

## 2026-11-07: Lite, design pass 2: the column from its data sheet, open wheel slots, pod springs, lidar

**Column mounting from LINAK's data sheet.** The first pass assumed a 130 × 120 mm top plate and an 8 mm foot bracket. The DL6 has neither: its top is its motor housing (177 × 97 × 46 mm, the column's axis 29.4 mm from one end, six M6 holes in its sides) and its outer profile ends in a bottom plate with 4 × M6 and 2 × M8. The column now bolts straight to an 18 mm foot plate (the bracket's height, so the column top and every height stay put), and is turned so its 80 mm side runs across the robot and the housing overhangs to the back, under the torso, not out to one side. The head plate sits on the housing, and two cheeks bolted to its long sides keep it on; a printed cover goes round the housing. The column's 7.5 kg is now split 2.5 kg on the base and 5.0 kg on the moving link (the motor and spindle ride at the top), by estimate. Lite: 39.96 kg bare, 40.60 kg with the shells; the stability figures moved by a few per cent (tables in the entry below).

**Floor plate: open wheel slots.** The wheel slots, 144 mm along the rim and 8 mm past the wheel's outer face, put their corners outside the 262 mm plate, so the rim outside each was a loose sliver and the plate was five pieces (found when its own stress check reported it unattached). The slots now run out through the rim. The four shell segments over the wheels lost the rim their hooks caught; their hooks moved to ±18.5° from the segment's centre, clear of the slot, and the slots shortened from ±74 to ±72 mm (6 mm clear of the wheel's chord). The plate checks at SF 3.5 or more on its own.

**Pod springs.** The first layout's 22 mm spring pocket was too short for any spring: the tab, 1.75 × as far from the pivot as the wheel, moves ±14 mm, and the smallest steel spring that stores the work over that travel (about 1.6 J) with the wire at a safe stress needs a solid length of about 17 mm or more. The spring tab moved down 24 mm, giving a 46 mm pocket, and the spring is music wire 1.8 mm on a 13 mm mean diameter, 11.6 active coils, 60 mm free: 12.5 N/mm at the wheel, 100 N at mid travel, 200 N at the bump stop, 782 MPa (42% of the wire's strength). `cad/lite_base.py --check` fails if a change breaks it. Alternative: an extension spring in the 70 mm under the tab (more room, but hooks are the weak point and it needed a new post).

**Lidar: a 360°, 12 m 2-D unit with an open ROS 2 driver** (RPLIDAR C1 class, about $100). Fits the existing 70 mm bracket.

**Isaac Lab: a speed-limited column.** Training showed the stiff implicit drive moving the column at 0.8 m/s (the motor's 0.038): the drive's velocity limit did not bind. Lite's column is now a `Spindle` actuator whose target moves at the rated speed, and `check_robot.py --variant lite` checks it (37 mm in 1 s). The reach task also stopped ending episodes as soon as the hand was within 5 cm (Isaac Lab's base reach task does): its policies never learned to hold a target.

## 2026-11-07: Lite, a second variant: the upper body on a lifting column on a wheeled base

**Decision.** The repository describes two robots. **Max** is the full humanoid and stays the default everywhere. **Lite** is Max from the waist motor up (waist, torso, arms, hands, neck, head), unchanged, on a bought standing-desk lifting column on a round omnidirectional base. Lite needs no legs, so it can be built and used for manipulation long before the walking robot.

**Base** (`cad/lite_base.py`, design pass 1, not built or tested): 0.55 m across, with four 152 mm double omni wheels in an X (at 45, 135, 225 and 315°, so all four drive forward). Each wheel is driven directly by an RS00 through a hub and carried at its outer face by a 6901 bearing in a second plate (the arm's yoke idea), and each pod is a sprung trailing arm. A 6 mm floor plate and a 4 mm deck plate hold the column at two levels 130 mm apart. The battery is split into two bricks in series at the sides, the electronics sit behind the column, a 2-D lidar in front and an e-stop on the deck. The shell is eight printed segments that snap onto the floor plate and pin into grommets in the deck. The base weighs 24.9 kg with the column; Lite about 39 kg.

**Column at the centre, facing forward.** Turning the torso 180° by default to reach further would cut the tipping margin to about 29 mm, and the waist turns only ±150°. A column set forward of the centre was rejected for the same reason.

**Stability, from `sim/mujoco/check_lite.py`** (Lite on its four wheels with free-turning rollers; 41.1 kg):

| Case | Column | Tipping margin | Braking limit | Sideways push that tips it, at the shoulders |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arms down | down / up | 163 mm | 2.4 / 1.6 m/s² | 66 / 46 N |
| Arms forward, 2 kg in each hand | down / up | 92 mm | 1.1 / 0.8 m/s² | 72 / 50 N |

The braking limit is what the base controller allows: 0.6 of the deceleration that would tip Lite statically, ramped in over 0.3 s. At 0.8 of it, the rear wheels unloaded with the column up and the arms forward, because the upper body sways forward on its joints' servos as the base slows. With the column up, Lite tips over at a sideways push of about 46 N at the shoulders, so the column comes down when people are close or Lite drives. Lite crosses a 10 mm bar sideways, an omni wheel's worst direction.

**Column: LINAK DL6.** 560 mm retracted and used over 450 mm of its 650 mm stroke (shoulder 1.01–1.46 m); 50 × 80 mm, 800 N, 38 mm/s, 7.5 kg, about $161, with a published dynamic bending rating of 150 N·m. The worst static moment at its foot is 52 N·m with the arms forward and 2 kg in each hand (SF 2.9), and about 60 N·m while braking at the limit (SF 2.5). It is driven through its own 24 V motor and Hall sensor by an H-bridge and a microcontroller, the way an open mobile-manipulator project drives a desk leg, not through LINAK's control box, whose interface is not public. That is the first thing to bench-test when the column arrives.

Alternatives: the TiMOTION TL18 (250 N·m, raw motor and Hall wiring documented, but 196 × 148 mm, about 12 kg and quote-only); the LINAK LC1 (250 N·m, 140 × 175 mm, about 12 kg); a hobby desk leg (cheap and proven by that project, but with no published section or bending rating). The TL18 is the fallback if the DL6 cannot be driven directly.

**Wheels: four omni wheels in an X.** Three wheels were rejected because a three-wheel base roughly halves the tipping margin at this size; mecanum wheels because they handle thresholds worse. Omni rollers slip, so odometry leans on the IMU and lidar.

**One description, two variants.** `description/robot.yaml` gets a `variants:` section. Lite is Max without the legs and pelvis, plus its base as the floating root, four wheel joints and a sliding column joint. The omni wheels' rollers are not in the URDF: the MuJoCo check adds them, and training can treat the base as ideal planar joints. The tools take `--variant lite` and write their own files next to Max's (`marvin_bot_lite.urdf`), so a change to the torso, arms or head reaches both robots.

**Skills on Lite.** The motion generator (Kimodo) draws people, not robots. For Lite it is used only for the upper body, with standing prompts. The base path and the column height are written directly, as a planner would command them, inside the tipping limits. Retargeting a walking person instead would rock the base with the hips' sway and drive the slow column (38 mm/s) up and down.

## 2026-11-03: the yoke arm, not the tube upper arm; an RS10P output-bearing bench test

**Decision.** The arm stays as the yoke version (2026-11-02): yokes with idlers at the shoulder roll and the elbow, and the shoulder yaw RS10P held by its output alone.

**Alternative.** A tube upper arm was prototyped (git branch `arm-v2-prototype`, design note `docs/design/arm_v2.md` on that branch): a 70 × 1.5 mm 6061 tube round the shoulder yaw motor, driven by a bulkhead on its output and turning on an acetal bushing round a collar at its rear end, so all three arm RS10Ps are held at both ends. It passed every check (lowest SF 3.5; 1.5 with the idlers carrying nothing; palm deflection under a 10 N side load 0.68 mm against 3.54 mm; 33 designed screws and dowels per arm; snap-fit PETG sleeves), but its structure is 0.10 kg heavier per arm (0.26 kg in all with its sleeves: 41.23 kg with shells against 40.97 kg) and it adds a tube, a collar and a bushing.

**Why.** The open question is whether the shoulder yaw motor's output bearing takes the arm's bending. RobStride publishes no bearing type or load rating for any of its actuators (none in the manuals or specification sheets revised 2026-07 to 2026-10; the STEP models are outer shells). The bending there is small in use and large only in a fall: static, from the arm's own 2.0 kg and a load in the hand, 4 N·m with the arm out level, 11 N·m with 2 kg in the hand, 22 N·m with 5 kg at full forward reach; about 38 N·m in the hand-catch case (0.5 × body weight at the palm). The hand catch is near the most the arm can apply: a larger push at the hand turns the shoulder pitch and roll motors past their peak torques (60 and 42 N·m), so they give way first, provided their torque limits are set; a joint against its end stop or a sharp impact can still exceed it.

**Bench test.** When the RS10Ps arrive: clamp one by its body, hang 10 kg on a bar 0.38 m from the output face (about 37 N·m) in a few orientations, and check the output for play and smooth rotation before and after. If it develops play, the tube upper arm is the fallback.

## 2026-11-02: arm yokes at the shoulder roll and elbow; shells in white PETG

**Why.** Each RS10P hung from a ring on its front face, so its own output bearing took the arm's bending. Under the hand-catch case (0.5 × body weight at the palm) that is about 38 N·m at the shoulder roll and the shoulder yaw and 9 N·m at the elbow, against a 42 N·m peak torque; RobStride's manual gives no bearing moment rating. A sideways fall onto the elbow (0.5 × body weight, a new case in `cad/fea.py`) gives 6-18 N·m; it governs no part.

**Yokes.** At the shoulder roll and the elbow the driven link now also rides on the motor's rear end: a hollow 7075 stub on the motor's rear face (its 4 × M3) carries a 6805-2RS ball bearing (25 × 37 × 7 mm, 22 g), and a plate on the driven link holds it, so a hand load reaches the stator through two bearings a motor length apart. The shoulder roll's plate stands on the roll link's top plate behind the motor; the elbow's plate joins the forearm frame by a box-section bridge under the motor, behind the forearm's centre line so it stays clear of the upper arm cover at 135°. `cad.covers --check` finds no contacts.

**Shoulder yaw: no yoke yet.** Its rear end is already bolted to the roll link's top plate (the stator side), so the upper arm cannot reach round to it without passing through the roll link. The options (a crossed-roller ring at its output, as at the hip yaw, or turning the motor over) are open.

**Thinned.** With the idler taking the arm's back end the roll link's front plate goes from 10 to 7 mm and the yaw ring from 4 to 3 mm. Because a ball-bearing idler with play may carry less than the check's rigid hold, `cad/fea.py --no-idler` reruns a link with the idlers carrying nothing; the thinned parts must pass the 2.2 target with the idler and stay at 1.5 or better without it (front plate: 10.8 and 1.6; yaw ring 10.7 and 4.8). The forearm frame stays 5 mm (the wrist yaw's torque governs it: 4 mm read SF 1.9); the upper arm and shoulder pitch parts carry the same load as before (the motor body still passes it to them). Net: +0.13 kg per arm (yokes, stubs and bearings 0.21 kg, thinning −0.08), 2.016 kg for both arms' structure.

**Shells in white PETG.** The body shells and head panels are printed in white PETG (density 1.27 against 1.10; carbon-fibre nylon only comes dark): shells 0.98 kg, head 0.499 kg. Robot 40.00 kg bare and 40.97 kg with shells, inside the 42 kg limit. Carbon-fibre nylon stays for hidden functional prints. The fastener and snap-fit pass follows.

## 2026-11-02: follow-ups to the arm and head changes

- **Shoulder pitch and waist yaw stay RS03.** Holding 1 kg at full forward reach takes 11.3 N·m at the shoulder pitch, over the RS10P's 9.5 N·m held rating; the waist carries little torque (99th percentile 6.3 N·m by inverse dynamics over the 25 reference clips) and would suit an RS10P, but its crossed-roller bearing and hub are built round the RS03 and the 0.44 kg it would save is not needed with 1.46 kg in hand under the limit.
- **Simulation:** the RS10P joints use RobStride's output-side inertia, 0.00625 kg·m², as their armature (`sim/isaaclab/marvin_bot/robot_cfg.py`, `sim/mujoco/check_standing.py`); the other armatures stay placeholders until the bench test.
- **Pelvis margin:** at a 4 mm mesh (a 3 mm mesh outgrows 200 GB) the pelvis back plate and the hip roll mounts check at SF 2.4, every other pelvis part at 2.6 or better; the 6 mm mesh had read 2.1-2.3.
- **Policies on the new model:** flat v9 (from flat v8) passes the MuJoCo cross-check; tracker v6 (from v5) keeps all 25 clips upright in Newton and MuJoCo and tracks closer than v5 (hands 5.6 cm against 6.7). The stair policy stays rough v9: it passes all 25 stair runs (5-20 cm, five noise seeds) on the current model, while the retrained rough v11 fails 3 of 5 on 20 cm stairs (`sim/isaaclab/README.md`).
- **Review fixes:** the RS10P's three front-face dowels are now cut in its rings (`actuators.yaml` gives their phase), and the stop and zero pins moved to 19° from a screw (the roll's to 79°) to clear them; the shoulder yaw's unused cable hole and two grommets went (its cable leaves the side), the rear-face bolts are counted in the actuator bolt list, and the upper-arm cover follows the yaw motor's 5 mm rise (40.55 kg with shells). Arm parts recheck at SF 2.6 or better (the elbow strap; it read 2.7 before the dowel holes). The head-drop case now loads the head's 0.467 kg at 10 g (46 N; it had 98 N).
- **RS10P margins seen in simulation:** the two-hand wave touches the shoulder yaw's 42 N·m peak, and the clap runs the shoulder roll at 78% of its 14 N·m turning rating (RMS); a 1 kg sideways hold (10.3 N·m) is over its 9.5 N·m held rating. Kept: short holds are covered by 14 N·m for 120 s, and the bench thermal test (phase 2) decides; an RS03 back at shoulder roll (+0.44 kg per arm) is the fallback.

## 2026-11-02: a rounded-box head with a visor band

The helmet head (the hull of two ellipsoids, a full-face glossy visor and a light camera frame across it) is replaced by the head drawn for the human-form study's approved exterior: a wide, deep rounded box, 156 mm wide, 203 mm deep and 121 mm tall, with a dark visor band across the face at eye height. `cad/head.py` draws it from that head's plan, side and front outlines (measured from its panels and kept as tables in the code), extruded across one another and intersected, every edge rounded 12 mm, a 1.8 mm wall, open underneath; it is printed as five light panels split where the study's were (crown, chin, two cheeks, a rear cap) and the visor band. The depth camera stays at eye height (92 mm over the neck pitch axis) and now sits in the middle of the band, its front 4 mm proud of the face on the centre line (24 mm further forward than before), on a longer arm from the head frame; its frame is dark, so it reads as part of the band. Level with the face, the band round its opening cut the colour camera's view by up to a fifth; 4 mm out, a ray cast from both IR cameras and the colour camera over the full 90 × 65° view clears 100%. The neck sleeve runs 5 mm up inside the head's open underside, which sits 29 mm over the pitch axis. The head's top is about 30 mm lower than the helmet's: the robot stands 1.41 m (requirement R1 updated). Head 0.467 kg (was 0.405); robot 40.54 kg with shells. `cad.covers --check` finds no contacts over the neck's range. The head link now has its interfaces (the pitch servo's horn and idler, the camera's tripod screw, the top bar the shell hangs from) and a 10 g head-drop case in `cad/fea.py`: every head frame part checks at SF 99 or more. That check found the camera arm had never joined the head frame (it stopped 4 mm under the bridge); the bridge now reaches down to it.

Alternatives considered (renders outside this repo): the study's head on the study's neck, with the neck pitch axis 72 mm higher, inside the head, over a longer dark neck (closest to the study, but it moves the neck joint); the same head 8% smaller; and keeping the helmet.

## 2026-11-02: RS10P at shoulder roll, shoulder yaw and elbow

The six arm motors from the shoulder roll down are now RobStride RS10P (42 N·m peak, 14 N·m rated turning and 9.5 N·m held still, 25:1, 0.46 kg, Ø57 × 60.6 mm; manual dated 2026-09-07 and specification sheet 2026-10-09), in place of the RS03 at shoulder roll and the RS06 at shoulder yaw and elbow. The human-form study (2026-10-31) named it worth carrying over; the motors come to 20.30 kg (was 21.82) and the robot to 39.64 kg bare and 40.47 kg with its shells (was 41.17 and 41.99), 1.5 kg under the limit. The arm parts end at 1.756 kg against 1.760 before. Parts cost rises about $140 (seven at $233.99 from a reseller listing, pre-order; RobStride lists it at ¥859 in China).

**Loads.** Holding torques from the URDF's masses (the arm alone, then 0.5 and 1 kg at the palm), against 9.5 N·m held still: the elbow and shoulder yaw need at most 4.5 N·m in any pose; the shoulder roll with the arm straight out sideways 5.7, 8.0 and 10.3 N·m (with the RS10P arm; 6.3, 8.6 and 10.9 with the old one). So the v1 hands' 0.5 kg can be held out sideways indefinitely, and the 1 kg of requirement R20 for about two minutes (the manual allows 14 N·m held for 120 s); forward reach is the shoulder pitch's, still an RS03. Peak torque rises at shoulder yaw and elbow (42 against 36 N·m) and falls at shoulder roll (42 against 60).

**Speed.** The 25:1 gear limits it to 13.1 rad/s at no load (14 N·m at 100 rpm, 3 at 120), against 50 rad/s for the RS06 and 20 for the RS03. The 32 retargeted reference clips peak at 12.8 rad/s at the elbow (a jog's arm swing) and 11.3 at the shoulder roll (one spike in an idle clip); the rest stay under 8. Walking and manipulation fit; a jog's arm swing would run at the motor's speed limit with little torque left. The URDF's velocity and effort limits follow, so the arm policies are to be retrained.

**Mounting.** The RS10P is a plain cylinder with no driver cap (its cable leaves the side near the rear), tapped on its front face (6 × M4 on Ø51, round the output's 6 × M4 on Ø27) and on its rear (4 × M3 on Ø38). It hangs from front rings like the RS06 (`cad/mounts.py`); `cad/arm.py` now takes each arm motor from `robot.yaml`. Being 10 mm longer than the RS06, the yaw and elbow motors sit 5 mm along their axes from their joints (`skeleton.yaml` motor_mounts), so their rings and outputs stay where the RS06's were and the forearm still folds to 135° clear of the shoulder (the joints, and so the kinematics, do not move). The yaw motor's rear face also bolts up to the shoulder roll link's top plate, which lets the cage strips behind it go; with a front screw circle half the RS06's, its ring needed its legs' pads joined across their full width (a neck between them read SF 0.2). The upper arm's ring on the yaw hub is 6 mm (the hub is Ø34, not Ø46), the elbow strap 5 mm, the yaw ring 4 mm, and the roll ring reaches the shoulder flange through a 38 mm tab inside the top plate's swing. Every arm part checks at SF 2.7 or better in `cad/fea.py` (the lowest the elbow strap in the hand catch; before, 2.5), and `cad.covers --check` finds no contacts.

Alternatives considered: RS10P at shoulder yaw and elbow only (−0.64 kg, the shoulder roll keeping its 60 N·m), and the shoulder pitch and waist yaw on RS10P too (both carry the most torque of their chains and were not checked here).

## 2026-10-31: a human-form frame studied; findings recorded, not yet applied to the description

A study asked whether the frame and actuator layout could be redrawn to fit a human body of Marvin Bot's height (a body generated with Anny), keeping the height, the actuator classes and the weight about the same. It drew on RP1's published layout (ideas only): ankle motors with their axes fore and aft, each turning a crank whose rod runs down the back of the shin to the heel; hip roll behind the hip; hip yaw low in the thigh; a waist roll under the waist yaw. The structure was flat, through-windowed aluminium plates cut to the body's outline, all 2.5D and millable on a desktop mill, joined by screws, with printed covers over each link that leave the actuators visible at the joints, and a plain helmet with a dark visor band across the camera (no facial features). The study code and renders are kept outside this repository; nothing in `description/` or `cad/` changed.

**Actuators.** RS04 at hip pitch, hip roll and knee; RS03 at hip yaw, waist roll, waist yaw and shoulder pitch; RS10P (42/14 N·m, 25:1, 0.46 kg, 125 rpm no-load, manual dated 2026-09-07) as the ankle pair, at shoulder roll, shoulder yaw and elbow; RS00 at the wrist; 19.1 kg of actuators against 21.5 kg today. With the RS10P put in a copy of the description, `check_standing.py` passes in all three poses and `check_step_up.py` finds an 18 cm step possible, but on one foot the hip yaw holds 16 N·m, 115% of the RS10P's rating, so hip yaw stays RS03. At the ankle (pitch ratio 1.3, roll ratio 0.86) the pair is rated 36 N·m in pitch and 24 N·m in roll, above the 22 and 14 N·m the earlier ankle sizing asked for; its speed is the open question (about 10 rad/s at the ankle at no load), which needs a walking policy to check. At shoulder yaw and elbow the RS10P beats today's RS06 on peak torque (42 against 36 N·m) and mass (0.46 against 0.62 kg).

**Strength.** `cad/fea.py`'s model and load cases on 13 links, the plates of each treated as bonded: every one checks at SF 2.2 or better at 44.5 kg (lowest about 2.3: the hip roll output, the torso in the hand catch, the shin pushed sideways at the ankle). Getting there needed what a plate frame lacks by default: a boxed pelvis (a keel with webs across it, a shelf to the waist roll carrier, clamp rings round the hip pitch housings), a front plate closing the shin, a web between the thigh plates, ribs in the third direction at both hip links (the landing's fore-aft moment otherwise bends every plate there out of its plane), ribs under the foot, and 7075 for the most stressed parts (4.1 of the frame's 14.0 kg). Every seam was then sized by hand as a screwed joint (M3 to M5 into blocks, tapped plate edges and tabs), each at 2 or more on slip and thread pull-out; the ankle motor mounts are lowest (2.1 on slip, so they also get dowels).

**Motion.** Swept joint by joint: full range at hip pitch, hip yaw, ankle pitch and roll, the waist, the neck, shoulder pitch and wrist yaw. The knee stops at 105° (130° wanted): past it the shin's lower end meets the hip yaw motor's block at the top of the thigh, a cost of mounting hip yaw low in the thigh. The elbow stops at 125° (135° wanted), hip roll at -10° with the other leg still, and with the arm hanging the hand meets the hip past 40° of shoulder yaw.

**Why it is not applied.** It is heavier, not lighter: about 42.7 kg bare and 44.5 kg with 1.4 mm covers, against today's 41.2 and 42.0 kg and the 42 kg limit, once the plates are strong enough. And the RS04s at the hip and knee do not fit inside a human-proportioned body of this height: the hip roll carrier stands up to 70 mm behind the body's hip and the knee and shin plates about 40 mm proud of it, so the covers would read as bulky there regardless. Worth carrying into today's design on their own: the arm layout (the upper arm as the outer fork round the elbow motor, a forearm that narrows to the wrist inside it, the elbow axis tipped by the carrying angle so the forearm can be flat plates), RS10P at shoulder yaw and elbow, and the covers' style. Alternatives considered: a body-shaped skin of printed panels held by magnets (rejected for a simpler covered look), RS10P at hip yaw (over its rating), RS05 at the wrist (5.5 N·m against the RS00's 14), and RS06 or RS03 pairs at the ankle (they do not fit between the thigh plates' 56 mm gap that the shin's contents must clear as the knee bends).

## 2026-10-31: harness clip points in the CAD; a faceted torso shell; the shells to be redesigned in build123d

The harness is now held at real points in the parts, not "a cable tie through a pocket". Each service loop is tied either side of its joint (`CLIPS` in `tools/build_harness.py`, checked against `description/cad_model.yaml`) by one of three clips: a bought push-in cable-tie mount in a 6.5 mm hole on a solid 14 mm pad, in a dozen plates up to 6 mm thick (`clip_spot` in `cad/b3d.py` puts each hole near its joint, outside the motor and its rings, and the pockets keep off the pad); a printed TPU edge clip that slides over the edge of a thicker plate (`cad/clips.py`; the pelvis back plate, the hip pitch box's outer wall, the shoulder roll front plate, the neck bridge); or a P-clip under an existing screw at the shoulder flange ([design/assembly_wiring.md](design/assembly_wiring.md)). The run lengths are measured from each RobStride's driver cap, where its cable leaves.

The shells were brought up to the new frame. The torso shell, a stack of rounded slices that read as ribbed, is now faceted: squashed octagons at nine heights, every other one turned 22.5°, each sized to hold the frame within 14% of the height either side, and hulled, so its flat panels meet in diamond facets (`faceted` in `cad/covers.py`). Its side walls are chamfered at the shoulders, and its front corners bevel deeper towards the shoulders, where the shoulder roll bracket swings past with the arm forward and rolled in. Arm shells over a RobStride housing get a grille of whole slots across its front, as the motors' ratings assume their housings can shed heat; the thigh and shin keep clean faces. Each shell has room cut for the harness loops at its joints: a band round the joint's axis over the arc between the loop's two clip points, plus 20° of swing, taking the rear arc where the two are near equal (at the knee the clips are above and below the axis, and a full ring had notched the front of the thigh shell). `cad.covers --check` finds no contacts. Mass 41.17 kg bare, 41.99 kg with the 0.82 kg of shells, 10 g under the limit. The pelvis back plate reads SF 2.1-2.3 between runs at the 6 mm mesh, so its margin needs firming up before it is made.

Styling options tried for the torso and legs (renders outside this repo): smooth rounded and chamfered lofts with a V, multi-faceted keels and sculpted chests, variations of the alternating-facet torso, a design language carried over the whole body (too bulky), and faceted or calmer thigh and shin panels at today's size. Only the faceted torso was kept. The shells remain generated: an offset hull of each link's parts, trimmed by flat cuts, so the frame sets their shape and it is hard to make them look designed. They will be redrawn in build123d as designed panels, lofted through cross-sections, given an even wall, filleted, split along chosen seams and fitted with clips, with today's hulls as the keep-out they must clear, starting with a thigh pilot. Alternatives considered: sculpting the surfaces in Blender (best for a sculpted look, but not parametric, so every frame change means redrawing), implicit (signed-distance) modelling (soft forms, hard to keep crisp edges), and graphical surfacing in FreeCAD (outside the scripted, CI-checked flow).

## 2026-10-30: RS03 and RS06 mounted by their output-end ears; bearings, zero pins and an all-aluminium load path

RobStride's drawings and STEP models show the RS03 and RS06 are tapped only in ears at their output end (2.5 and 1.5 mm behind the output face; the RS03's rear has 4 tapped holes, the RS06's none found), and the RS00 on its front face; only the RS04 is tapped on both faces (`description/actuators.yaml`: `face`, `setback`). The CAD had bolted every motor through its rear face, into holes that are not there on all 15 RS03/RS06 positions. Each RS03 and RS06 now hangs from a ring on its ears (`cad/mounts.py`: `front_ring`), and the part it drives stands on a hub on the output, past the ring (`hub`); where the structure is on the motor's rear side, flat strips round the motor join the ring to it (`cage`). The ankle motors bolt straight to the shin plate their output passes through. The hip yaw motor moved onto the thigh (output up, `skeleton.yaml` motor_mounts).

Thin crossed-roller bearings (RA6008, 60 x 76 x 8 mm, about C0 8.7 kN as distributors list it: to confirm with the maker's sheet) sit in the rings at the hip yaw (static safety factor about 2.1 in the 3 g landing, with 1.2 kN axial and 112 N·m tilt), the waist, and in a carrier on each hip roll RS04's front face, so those joints' tilting loads reach the motor housing or the frame without passing through the motors' unrated output bearings. With the carrier, the hip roll yoke's rear bearing, plate, strip and stub went. ## 2026-10-30: finite-element check of every load-carrying part; the structure is under-designed

`cad/strength.py` cuts sections along a load path and uses beam theory without torsion, so it covered 13 load paths and left out every motor ring, flange, crank and torso frame. `cad/fea.py` now checks them all: each link's metal parts (and the printed shells that carry load between them) meshed as one bonded solid, held at the face of the motor or bearing that carries the link, loaded through a rigid coupling at the next link's interfaces, with the strength check's load cases (a 3 × body-weight landing on one leg with 0.5 and 0.3 × body weight across it, a 0.5 × body-weight hand catch, a sideways fall onto the knee, each joint's peak torque). Each motor ring, flange, bearing bore and stub records the joint it serves and its contact patch (`Part.extra["ifaces"]`). Checked against beam theory in bending and thin-strip torsion (`tests/test_fea.py`), it reads 5-9% low at a 3 mm mesh, so its target is SF 2.2 on yield; stresses are smoothed along the surface over about a fillet's size, since the parts have no fillets yet. It takes about an hour, so it runs by hand after CAD changes, not in CI.

Result (worst case per part, SF on yield): the ankle cranks (9.7), ankle cross (34), foot (2.2 and up), torso base, flange and battery shelf (2.7-7), forearm flange (4.1), hip pitch flange (4.1) and neck bracket (15) pass. The rest fail, for two reasons the beam check could not see. **Torsion of thin plates:** the fore-aft part of the landing twists the hip roll bracket's front and stator plates (SF 0.2-0.4) and the shin plates (0.8-0.9); the hand catch twists the shoulder pitch bracket's plates (0.6). **Motor rings on three posts:** every rear ring carries its joint's whole load through three Ø8 mm posts (hip pitch ring 0.5, hip yaw ring 1.3, shoulder roll ring 0.7), and the pelvis carries one leg's landing into the torso through the waist motor's ring (pelvis back plate and gussets 0.1, waist and hip roll rings 0.2-0.4). Also near: thigh plate 1.3 (side fall), hip pitch box 1.2-1.3, torso shoulder plate and neck bridge 1.1. It also found CAD faults: the shin spacers are 2 mm short of the plates (sized with the 6 mm plate thickness, not the shin's 4 mm), the upper arm's printed shell does not touch its two rings, and the computer plate is attached to nothing; and the printed forearm shell reads SF 0.3 against the beam check's 7.2, which needs a closer look. So the parts need strengthening, not thinning, before they are made: closed sections or ribs where plates twist, and rings bolted straight to plates (or on a continuous wall) instead of posts, which also rules out the bought-standoff posts considered in the parts review. Alternatives considered: refine the beam check with torsion (still blind to rings and junctions); CalculiX through gmsh (gmsh could not rebuild several links' surfaces; fTetWild meshes any closed surface).

Each ankle uses two RS03 motors mounted high on the calf, driving pitch and roll through push rods with spherical rod ends, which keeps the shin light. Hip axis to sole is 0.69 m (55% of height), sized for 17–18 cm stairs.
Alternatives: knee motor near the hip with a linkage (lower leg inertia, more parts; revisit if simulation shows a need); serial ankle pitch and roll motors (heavier shin).

## 2026-10-25: arms use RS06 at shoulder yaw and elbow

Robstride's RS06 (36 N·m peak, 0.621 kg) replaces the RS02 (17 N·m). Roboto Origin uses 27 N·m-peak actuators at every arm joint; Asimov 1's simulation model limits shoulder yaw to 20 N·m and the elbow to 12 N·m. The RS02's 17 N·m peak is below the first, and the RS06 leaves margin for push-ups and getting up after a fall. Cost +~¥600 for four, mass +~0.96 kg. Revisit with simulated arm torques in phase 1.

## 2026-10-25: Robstride actuators, RS04 / RS03 / RS06 / RS00

Robstride gives the most torque per dollar of the quasi-direct-drive actuators compared, publishes manuals and specifications in a public repository, and has the best open software support among budget brands (Python, Rust and ROS 2 drivers). RS04 (120 N·m) at hip pitch, hip roll and knee; RS03 (60 N·m) at hip yaw, ankles, waist and shoulder pitch and roll; RS06 at shoulder yaw and elbow; RS00 at the wrists. Second source for the 120 N·m class: Damiao DM-J10010L (also MIT-mode CAN). Ask RobStride for a direct quote: China list prices are roughly half of US reseller prices before shipping and tariffs.
Alternatives: CubeMars (2–3× the price per N·m), Damiao (fewer verified specs), Encos and MyActuator (several times the price), self-built cycloidal actuators (more work, lower torque).

## 2026-10-25: six motor CAN buses plus one battery bus

Robstride uses classic CAN 2.0B with 29-bit identifiers at 1 Mbit/s, and each motor needs a command and a reply frame per cycle. Six motors on one bus at 500 Hz would load it to roughly 80–90%. Each leg gets two buses (hip: 3 motors; shank: knee and two ankle motors) at 500 Hz; each arm gets one bus at 250 Hz, with the waist on the right arm's bus. All stay at or below 48% load (worst-case 160-bit frames). A seventh bus carries battery telemetry. The Orin's two on-board CAN controllers (with 3.3 V transceivers) and isolated USB-CAN adapters (gs_usb, SocketCAN) provide the buses; a microcontroller bridge is the fallback if bench tests show too much jitter.

## 2026-10-25: power: 13S3P quick-release pack, keep-alive supply, 4.1 V charging

A 13S3P pack of 21700 high-discharge cells (~630 Wh, ~3.4 kg) gives about 1.5× the runtime of 13S2P for ~1.1 kg more. It sits on a quick-release cradle with an anti-spark blind-mate connector. A small keep-alive battery feeds only the computer through an ideal-diode OR, so the computer stays up during a swap. A full 13S pack is 54.6 V against the actuators' 60 V limit, so regen spikes are a real risk: charge to 4.1 V per cell (53.3 V) for daily use and fit a clamp at about 56–58 V. DC-DC converters are rated for 75 V input, and the computer's supply is wired upstream of the motor contactor so an e-stop never reboots it. Main path: Class-T fuse, precharge, contactor.
Alternatives: 13S2P (lighter, ~1 h); two packs OR-ed for swapping while walking (v2).

## 2026-10-25: sensing: active-stereo depth camera, low-latency IMU

Head camera: Orbbec Gemini 335L. It computes depth on the camera, leaving the Orin's GPU free; its IR pattern gives depth on plain floors and carpeted stairs; it connects over USB-C; and Isaac Sim 6.2 has a built-in model of it. Driver support on JetPack 7.2 must be confirmed before ordering.
IMU: the locomotion policy needs a low-latency gyro at 500 Hz or more. A low-cost USB AHRS module tops out around 200 Hz, so it is the backup; the primary will be an industrial AHRS or a BMI088 read by a microcontroller, decided after a bench test.
Alternatives: ZED X Mini (needs a capture card and computes depth on the GPU), RealSense D455 (USB stability reports on Jetson).

## 2026-10-25: compute and software stack

Onboard: Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB developer kit (already available), JetPack 7.2, Isaac ROS 4.6 and ROS 2 Jazzy. Isaac ROS 5.0 is skipped for now because of reported regressions on Orin. A real-time control process (CAN I/O, 50 Hz policy, safety) runs outside ROS. Training: Isaac Lab 3.0 with rsl_rl, MuJoCo as a cross-check. Manipulation: GR00T N1.7, served from a desktop GPU first and moved to the Orin with TensorRT later. Whole-body control starts with the RL lower body plus IK arms; a motion-tracking controller (retargeting with GMR, BeyondMimic-style training) comes later. NVIDIA's SONIC checkpoints are for another robot and need far more compute to retrain, so it is deferred.
Alternatives: Jetson Thor T4000 (same module price in 2026; worth considering for a second robot).

## 2026-10-25: vendor CAD is linked, not committed
