Playground 17

Motion tracking: Kimodo, retargeting and BeyondMimic

Instead of writing a new reward for every skill, Marvin Bot learns one policy (a trained neural-network controller, page 16) that copies a reference motion. A new skill is then a new clip. This page follows a clip from a text prompt to a trained tracker, and lets you break the two steps that most often go wrong: scaling a human to a 1.44 m robot, and the shape of the reward.

Think of a dance class: rather than explaining in words what a good bow looks like, the teacher does it and you copy. A humanoid is expected to do many things besides walking, such as waving, bowing, squatting or clapping, and writing a separate scoring rule for each would take weeks per skill. With motion tracking the robot is shown a recording and scored on how closely it copies it. The hard parts are getting a good recording for a body that is not human-shaped, and deciding what "close enough" means.

The idea in one picture

For walking (page 16) the reward is hand-written: follow the commanded velocity, keep the body upright, do not waste torque, lift the feet. That works for one skill, but every new skill (a bow, a squat, a wave) needs new reward terms and weeks of tuning. Motion tracking turns the problem around: give the policy a reference motion, a recording of where every body part should be at every moment, and reward it for staying close. The reward is the same for every skill; only the clip changes.[1]

1 · GenerateKimodo turns a text prompt and a root path into a human motion (SOMA skeleton, 30 fps).[2]
→
2 · RetargetPyRoki solves for Marvin Bot joint angles whose body points match the scaled human keypoints.[3]
→
3 · Checkcheck_clips.py: text match, foot sliding, per-skill geometry, review renders.
→
4 · TrackOne policy learns all clips with BeyondMimic's rewards in ProtoMotions on Newton.[1][4]

Words in the pipeline: Kimodo is a diffusion model, a neural network that generates new motions (much as image generators make pictures from text). SOMA is the human skeleton it outputs; 30 fps means 30 poses (frames) per second. A root path is the route the hips should follow; keypoints are tracked points on the body such as wrists, knees and ankles.

Two of these steps hide most of the difficulty. Retargeting has to turn a 1.75 m person into a 1.44 m robot with different proportions and joint limits, and every compromise it makes becomes part of the "truth" the policy is trained to copy. The tracker's reward has to say how close is close enough. The two playgrounds below let you try both.

From first principles

Why a reference motion?

Reinforcement learning (page 16) needs a number, the reward, that says how good each moment was. For walking, people write it by hand. A tracker's reward is generic: "be where the reference is". BeyondMimic showed that one such formulation, with one set of hyperparameters, learns sprints, cartwheels and dances and transfers to a real humanoid without per-skill tuning.[1] The price is that you need good reference motions, and that the robot must be physically able to do them.

Step 1: making motions from text (Kimodo)

Kimodo is NVIDIA's kinematic motion diffusion model, trained on about 700 hours of commercially usable optical motion capture. It takes a text prompt and optional constraints (full-body keyframes, hand or foot positions, a 2D root path or waypoints) and outputs a human motion on NVIDIA's SOMA skeleton.[2][5]

Go deeper: Kimodo's licences

Its code is Apache-2.0; the SOMA "RP" model is under the NVIDIA Open Model License; its text encoder (the part that turns the prompt into numbers) is Meta's Llama 3 8B Instruct (gated, Meta Llama 3 Community License) with LLM2Vec adapters.

"Kinematic" means it outputs positions and angles only, not forces: nothing guarantees the motion is physically possible, which is why the clips are checked.

Wording alone does not set speed. In the repo, "a person jogs forward" produced a 2 m/s run that no tracker could follow. So every locomotion clip and in-place skill gets a root path: the hips' speed, heading and direction of travel frame by frame (generate_kimodo.py). Several samples are generated per clip (4 by default, 8 for the clips that needed it) and the one with the fewest failed skill checks, then the least foot sliding and crossing of the feet, is kept.

Step 2: retargeting (PyRoki)

The robot has different segment lengths, fewer joints (no waist pitch, no wrist roll) and joint limits. Retargeting finds, frame by frame, the robot joint angles q that best put the robot's body points on the human's keypoints (pelvis, hips, knees, ankles, toes, shoulders, elbows, wrists). PyRoki writes this as a least-squares problem (add up the squared misses and make the total as small as possible) and solves it with a Levenberg–Marquardt optimiser, a standard method that repeatedly nudges the angles downhill, written in the JAX numerical library:[3]

minimise over q: Σk wk ‖FKk(q) − s ⊙ pk‖² + wlim·limits(q) + wcoll·self-collision(q) + wrest‖q − qrest‖² + smoothness

Here FKk(q) is forward kinematics: the position of robot body point k for joint angles q; pk is the human keypoint; s is the scale (⊙ means each direction can be scaled separately); the w are weights saying how much each wish matters; ‖…‖² is the squared distance. In words: put the robot's points on the shrunk human's points, stay inside the joint limits, do not let limbs pass through each other, stay near a resting pose, and move smoothly.

The scale is the hard part. Shrink everything by the height ratio (1.438/1.75 = 0.82) and the human's leg is still 0.70 m long while Marvin Bot's straight leg is 0.640 m: either the feet float or the pelvis must drop. Shrink by too much and the robot has to crouch. Arms, torso and legs each need their own scale, which is what the playground's "Per body part" mode does. GMR, a retargeter built for exactly this problem, also uses non-uniform local scaling followed by a two-stage optimisation, and its authors show that retargeting artefacts (foot sliding, self-penetration) directly hurt the trained tracker.[6]

What the playground's solver does, exactly

Human proportions come from the repo's measured source (hip-to-ankle 0.853 m and shoulder-to-wrist 0.56 m for a 1.75 m person) plus standard anthropometry for the rest. Each frame, the human's joint angles give its keypoints; they are scaled (uniformly about the floor point under the hips, or legs about the floor, shoulders about the hips, arms about each shoulder, as in scale_keypoints()). The robot pelvis is pinned to the scaled pelvis target with the human's pelvis tilt. Each leg is solved analytically (hip roll, hip pitch, knee) to reach its ankle target, then clamped to its limits; the ankle pitch keeps the foot flat within ±40°. Each arm (shoulder pitch, roll, yaw, elbow) is solved by 30 damped Gauss–Newton steps on wrist error plus 0.3 × elbow error plus a small pull to the human's angles; it is solved once without limits (to find what the motion asks for) and once with limits, including the coupled shoulder-roll limit. Simplifications: no self-collision, no smoothness term, no root optimisation (PyRoki moves the root too), and a 2-link leg instead of the full chain.

Step 3: checking the clips

A bad clip wastes hours of training and teaches the wrong thing, so sim/motion/check_clips.py runs four layers, the first three on Kimodo's human clip and the third again on the retargeted robot clip, so a failure points at generation or at retargeting:

  1. Text match. NVIDIA's text–motion retrieval model for SOMA (TMR-SOMA-RP-v1, the model Kimodo's benchmark scores with) turns every clip and every prompt into a list of numbers (an "embedding") placed so that a motion and a sentence describing it land close together. Each clip gets its similarity to its own prompt (cosine mapped to 0–1) and that prompt's rank among all 25. A clip that another prompt describes better is suspect.
  2. Motion quality. Kimodo's benchmark metrics: foot sliding from toe height and from Kimodo's contact labels, the share of contact frames that skate, contact consistency, and how far the hips strayed from the requested root path. Kimodo's published foot skate is 2–4 cm/s.
  3. Skill checks. A geometric test per clip, in units of the standing head height H so it runs on both bodies: the hands meet (closer than 0.07 H) in front of the chest at least three times in a clap; a hand above the shoulder 40% of the time and swinging in a wave; the pelvis dropping by 30% in a squat; distance within ±25% and turn within 25° of the root path for a walk; both feet off the ground in 5% of jog frames, and so on.
  4. Review renders. The human as a stick figure beside the robot reference, with the checks written on the frame.

On the first 25 clips every clip ranked its own prompt first or second and slid 0.2–3.8 cm/s, but five did less than asked (the wave waved for 2 s of 8; the arm raise reached about 45°). Those five were regenerated with sharper prompts and more samples, now picked by the skill checks, and all 25 pass on the human. On the robot two fall short: the clap's palms meet once rather than three times, and the pick-up's hands stop 27 cm above the floor, because with no waist pitch the robot cannot bend over.

Step 4: the tracker's reward (BeyondMimic)

The tracker observes its own joint angles, joint velocities, IMU (the inertial sensor in the pelvis) and last action, plus the reference: the target pose at the next few frames (ProtoMotions uses 1, 2, 4 and 8 frames ahead) and the error of an anchor body (the torso) against the reference's anchor.[1][4] Every other body's target is expressed relative to the anchor, so a small drift in where the robot stands does not count as a pose error. Each tracking term is an exponential kernel, a scoring curve that pays 1 for a perfect match and fades towards 0 as the error grows:

r(ē, σ) = exp(−ē / σ²), ē = mean over bodies of ‖xrobot − xref‖²

ē is a mean squared error (m² for positions, rad² for orientations), and σ (sigma) is the "tolerance": an error of one σ gives exp(−1) = 0.37 of the reward, two σ gives 0.02. In BeyondMimic σ is 0.3 m for body positions, 0.4 rad for orientations, 1.0 m/s for linear and 3.14 rad/s for angular velocities, each with weight 1.0, plus optional anchor position and orientation terms with weight 0.5.

Penalties are −10 for soft joint limits (0.9 of the mechanical range), −0.1 for action rate and −0.1 for undesired contacts.[1]

An episode (one attempt) ends when the anchor or an end effector (a hand or foot) is 0.25 m too high or low, or the anchor is tilted 0.8 rad from the reference. Training starts episodes at random points of the clips, more often in the one-second bins where the policy has been failing (adaptive sampling, a moving average of failures with a uniform floor).[1]

The kernel's shape matters. Its slope, dr/de = −(2e/σ²)·r, is zero at e = 0 and largest at e = σ/√2. With σ = 0.3 m, a body 3 cm off keeps exp(−0.03²/0.3²) = exp(−0.01) = 0.990 of its reward and the gradient is tiny. Playground B shows what that does.

Playground B: the reward kernel

How strict should the score be? Here you set the tolerance σ and an error, and see how much reward the robot keeps and how strongly the score pushes it to improve (the slope: zero slope means "no reason to fix this"). Start here: press 3 cm crouch and read Reward lost: well under 1%, too little for training to bother fixing. Then press the other scenarios. How to read the chart: the solid blue curve is the reward if every scored body is off by the error on the x-axis; the dashed purple curve is the same when only some of them are off (the average dilutes the miss); the orange dot is your current setting. A flat curve under the dot means the robot feels almost no pull towards the reference.

How Marvin Bot does it

In the repo One tracker follows all 25 Kimodo clips on the 1.44 m model: trained for 4,800 epochs with 16,384 robots at once (an epoch is one round of collecting experience and updating the network; batch 65,536, about 5.6 s an epoch, about 7.5 hours on one RTX-class GPU), and every clip stays upright. Joint errors are 8–13° RMS on most clips and the hands 3–9 cm from the reference's; the two-hand wave is the weakest (34.6°, 12.1 cm). Nothing has been replayed in MuJoCo or run on hardware yet. These results are provisional: a new tracker (v4, with the head and joint-angle rewards below) is still training on the regenerated clips, so the per-clip numbers will change. Sources: sim/motion/README.md, docs/skills.md, docs/decisions.md (2026-10-27 to 2026-10-29).

What Marvin Bot changed from upstream's BeyondMimic config in ProtoMotions, and why (each was found by a failed tracker):

  • Scale. Legs by leg length, shoulders about the hips (0.62 as wide, about 0.955 as high, 6.5 cm forward), arms about each shoulder; the knees and feet moved 1.5 and 3 cm outwards because the shrunk human feet ended up 12–14 cm apart and Marvin Bot's feet are 11 cm wide. With the shoulders or pelvis left high, the solver crouched the robot (knees 37–58° through a walk). The solver also starts from the standing pose, not the middle of each joint's range (a 65° knee).
  • Arms kept out of the body. Upstream leaves the self-collision cost off; retargeted hands went 4–10 cm into the thighs in most walking frames and the palms 9 cm through each other in the clap. retarget_marvin_bot.py turns it on (weight 10, margin 2 cm) for the arms against the trunk, legs and the other arm.
  • Soles. ProtoMotions' converter puts the lowest body origin 4 cm above the floor, which suits another robot's foot frame. fix_sole_height.py lifts each clip until the flatter (stance) foot is on the floor; lifting until the single lowest corner touched left walks 3–5 cm in the air, and the trackers hopped to keep up.
  • Anchor reward and termination. BeyondMimic's global anchor-position reward (weight 0.5, σ 0.3 m) and a "fell behind" termination at 0.5 m were missing upstream; without them trackers 1–4 stood still.
  • Contact penalty −2.0 per foot whose contact differs from the reference's. Without it the trackers hopped (both feet down 70% of the time, both up 20%); −0.5 only halved it; at −2.0 both feet are up 1–2% of the time.
  • Fifteen scored bodies: BeyondMimic's 14 key bodies plus the head. Scoring all 28 let an arm hang while the reference raised it (hands 40–75 cm off); leaving the head out made it shake (46–60°/s RMS standing still).
  • Joint-angle term, weight 0.5, σ 0.3 rad, exp(−mean squared joint error/σ²) over all joints. The body-position kernel paid 99% for a body 3 cm off, so the tracker stood and walked 5–8° deeper in the hips, knees and ankles than the reference, and with an arm overhead chose a different arm posture for the same hand position (shoulder yaw 95° off in the two-hand wave).
  • Looser velocity term (σ 0.5 m/s instead of 1.0), more exploration (log std −2.3), softer gains for an action scale of at least 0.4 rad, and an episode end when any scored body is 25 cm from the reference.
  • Adaptive sampling in ProtoMotions is per clip: a clip's sampling weight goes back to 1 when the policy fails it and decays by 0.999 per evaluation when it succeeds (BeyondMimic's own version works on one-second bins within a clip).
  • A second check. ProtoMotions counts a clip as tracked when the torso height stays within 25 cm; check_tracking.py also compares distance covered, joint angles and the hands and feet alone. That is how the first tracker's shuffling on the spot (0–32% of the reference's distance) showed up.

Where sources and the repo disagree
  • Arm and leg scale. sim/motion/README.md says the legs scale by 0.69 and the arms by 0.79, and a code comment says "0.79 for the 0.443 m reach of a 50 mm shoulder drop". The same script computes its scales from the current description: (0.325 + 0.315)/0.853 = 0.750 for the legs and (0.025 + 0.200 + 0.188 + 0.005)/0.56 = 0.746 for the arms, since the shoulder-roll offset in skeleton.yaml is now 25 mm. The README numbers appear to date from the 1.29 m body; the playground's "Marvin Bot today" uses the computed values. Which values the trained 25-clip tracker's references used is not recorded.
  • Shoulder height scale. The README says "0.75 high"; the code multiplies 0.75 by the ratio of the robot's shoulder-above-hip height to the source's, which is 0.955 on the 1.44 m body.
  • Samples per clip. docs/decisions.md (2026-10-28) says the best of four samples is kept, by foot sliding and crossing. The current script keeps the sample with the fewest failed skill checks first, then the least sliding, crossing and jerk, and the README says 8 samples were used for some clips.
  • Retargeter. The 2026-10-25 compute decision names GMR for retargeting; the implemented pipeline uses PyRoki.
  • BeyondMimic vs ProtoMotions sampling. The paper samples one-second bins inside each clip by failure rate; ProtoMotions' evaluator re-weights whole clips. Both are called adaptive sampling.

Decisions you can make here

  • How to scale: by height, by limb length, or per body part. Per body part keeps the feet on the floor and the knees straight, at the cost of changing the motion's proportions.
  • Which joint limits to relax (the coupled shoulder roll is already relaxed with the arm forward) and which skills to drop because the body cannot do them (no waist pitch: no picking things off the floor).
  • Reward tolerances: tighter σ for precise poses vs looser for faster early learning; whether to add terms (joint angles, contacts) or a style discriminator (a second network that scores whether a motion "looks human"). Each extra term fixes one failure but adds a weight to tune and another hours-long training run to test it.
  • How many bodies to score, and whether hands deserve their own term. Fewer bodies make each one count more (no hanging arm) but leave the unscored ones free to do odd things (the shaking head).
