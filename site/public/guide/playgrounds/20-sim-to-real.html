Sim-to-real and system identification

Marvin Bot's walking policies live in a simulator whose motors are educated guesses. Before they can run on metal, the guesses have to be measured, and the policy has to be trained to cope with whatever the measurements still get wrong. This page is about that gap and the two tools for closing it: system identification and domain randomisation.

~35 minBefore this: 04 Actuators, 15 PD control, 16 RL for walking
You will learn
  • What the sim-to-real gap is made of for a legged robot: actuator dynamics, latency, friction, contact, sensor noise, mass errors.
  • How system identification turns bench data into model parameters with least squares, and why the test signal matters.
  • How domain randomisation, actuator networks and privileged training cover what identification cannot.
  • What Marvin Bot's phase 2 bench will measure, and where each number goes back into the simulation.

The idea in one picture

A simulator is a set of equations with numbers in them: masses, inertias, friction, motor strength, delays. A policy trained in it learns to exploit those exact numbers. If the real robot's numbers differ, the policy's actions have different effects than it expects, and a gait that was balanced becomes unbalanced. That difference is the reality gap.[1][4] Two things shrink its effect: measure the real numbers and put them in the simulator (system identification), and vary the numbers in training so the policy works across a range that contains the real ones (domain randomisation).[2][3]

An everyday comparison: a learner who has only ever driven one car stalls a different car with a heavier clutch. You fix that either by telling them exactly how this clutch feels (measure it), or by having them practise on many different cars, so no clutch surprises them (randomise). Real projects do both. This is the step between the simulation work of pages 14–17 and building hardware (page 22, phase 2).

How to read the picture below: each panel is a range of values for one physical parameter (say, a motor's friction). The blue shape is the range of values the simulator trains on; the orange line is the real robot's value. Left: the guess misses reality. Middle: measuring moves the guess onto it. Playground: identify a motor, then deploy

You play the engineer at the phase 2 test bench. Hidden inside the page is a "true" motor (one joint with a test arm bolted to its output), standing in for the real hardware. In three steps:

  1. Test it. The bench sends the motor a pattern of current (current makes torque) and records its speed, with sensor noise: the grey trace in the top chart. A chirp is a wiggle that gets faster and faster.
  2. Match it. Move the five model sliders until your model's speed (blue) lies on the measured one, or press Fit to let least squares do it (it also tries every delay from 0 to 20 ms and keeps the best). The red residual under the chart is the mismatch: you want it as small as the noise.
  3. Use it. The bottom chart runs a leg-like motion on the joint with a PD controller that relies on your model to know how much torque to add. It compares what the simulator predicted with what the "real" joint does. A poor model shows up as a gap between the two.

Model and bench are a teaching toy: one joint, no gravity, no backlash; the true values are illustrative, not RobStride measurements.

From first principles

Where the gap comes from

SourceWhat the simulator assumesWhat reality does
Actuator dynamicsan ideal torque source, or a DC-motor curve with fixed armature and frictioncurrent loop bandwidth, gear friction that changes with load and temperature, backlash, torque constant off the datasheet, and torque that drops as the windings heat[5]
Latencycommands act instantlyinference time, CAN round trips and sensor filtering add milliseconds; a delay in a feedback loop removes phase margin[6]
Contact and frictionrigid bodies, one friction coefficientsoft soles, varying floors, slip[4]
Sensorsexact joint angles, exact orientationnoise, bias, IMU drift, encoder quantisation
Mass and geometryCAD masses and inertiascables, fasteners, glue, battery position: the real robot is heavier in places the CAD forgot

Tan et al. traced most of the gap on a small quadruped to two items: the actuator model and latency. Identifying the motor and adding the measured latency to the simulator was what made their gaits transfer.[6] Hwangbo et al. found the actuator so hard to model analytically that they learned it instead.[5]

A model of one joint

The playground's actuator obeys Newton's second law for rotation, the same model the bench step test fits:

J · dω/dt = Kt · i(t − d) − b · ω − τc · sign(ω)
  • J (kg·m²): inertia at the output. A geared motor's rotor inertia appears multiplied by the gear ratio squared: this "reflected inertia" is what simulators call armature. For a 9:1 gearbox a rotor of 4×10⁻⁴ kg·m² looks like 0.032 kg·m² at the output, larger than many leg links.
  • Kt (N·m/A): torque constant at the output; current i times Kt is the motor torque.
  • b (N·m·s/rad): viscous friction, proportional to speed (oil, eddy currents, back-EMF in the current loop).
  • τc (N·m): Coulomb friction, a constant drag opposing motion (gear teeth, seals). Units check: every term is N·m.
  • d (s): the delay between commanding a current and the torque appearing, including bus time.

System identification by least squares

The idea in plain words: write down the motor equation once for every sample of bench data, then pick the values of J, b and τc that make all those equations as nearly true as possible at once. That is what "least squares" means: the smallest total of squared mismatches.

The model is linear in its unknowns once the delay is fixed: rearranged, each sample k gives one equation

Kt i(tk − d) = J · αk + b · ωk + τc · sign(ωk)

where αk is the acceleration estimated by differentiating the smoothed speed. Thousands of samples give thousands of equations in three unknowns; least squares picks the J, b, τc that minimise the sum of squared equation errors, by solving the 3 × 3 "normal equations" (AᵀA)x = Aᵀy.[7] The delay enters non-linearly, so the Fit button repeats the solve for every delay from 0 to 20 ms in 0.5 ms steps and keeps the best. Kt cannot be separated from J, b and τc using speed data alone (doubling all four changes nothing), so it comes from a separate locked-output test: hold the output against a load cell, step the current, and fit the slope of torque against current.

Two practical lessons. First, the test signal must excite every parameter: steps reach steady speeds (where torque balances friction), chirps sweep frequencies (where inertia dominates). Second, noise matters little if you have many samples, but bias matters a lot: if the model lacks a term that reality has (backlash, say), least squares will distort the other parameters to compensate.

Mitigations, from cheapest to most involved

  • Observation noise and delay modelling: add realistic noise and latency to the simulated sensors and commands.[6]
  • Domain randomisation: sample physics parameters per episode so the policy must work across all of them. Peng et al. randomised dynamics such as masses, friction, damping and latency and gave the policy memory so it could adapt within an episode[2]; Tobin et al. introduced the idea for camera images[3]. Wider is more robust but more conservative.[8]
  • System identification: measure, then centre the randomisation on the measurement.[6][7]
  • Actuator networks: record a real actuator's response to many commands and train a small network that predicts torque from the recent history of position error and velocity; use it inside the simulator in place of the ideal motor.[5]
  • Privileged training and adaptation: a teacher policy sees the true parameters in simulation; a student learns to act from sensor history alone, implicitly estimating them.[9][10] History-conditioned humanoid policies do this in context.[11]
  • Learning the residual from real data: for humanoids, ASAP trains a correction ("delta action") model on real-world rollouts and fine-tunes the policy in the corrected simulator.[12]
  • Sim-to-sim checks: run the trained policy in a second, independent simulator before hardware; Humanoid-Gym uses MuJoCo for this.[13] A bipedal stair policy trained with randomisation transferred to hardware blind in Siekmann et al.[14]

How Marvin Bot does it

In short: today the simulator's motor numbers are educated guesses, softened by some randomisation and a second-simulator check. Phase 2 buys two real motors, measures them on a bench and puts the measured numbers back into the simulator, before most of the motors are bought. That order means a surprise costs two motors, not twenty-three.

In the repo Phase 2 in docs/plan.md: "One RS04 and one RS03, CAN adapter, bench supply with regen clamp. Measure torque constant, speed, heating, CAN timing, built-in position control; fit actuator models and feed them back into simulation", pass check "manual figures confirmed; models match measurements; CAN at the planned rates works on the Orin", 2–3 weeks. Scripts in bench/actuator/bench_robstride.py (untested until the motors arrive), procedure in bench/README.md, driver in robot/drivers/robstride.py.

Today: placeholders, a cross-check and some randomisation

  • Motor model: robot_cfg.py uses a DC-motor model per joint (torque saturates at the peak rating and falls linearly to zero at no-load speed) with gains, armature and friction that its docstring calls "placeholders until the phase 2 bench test fits them": hip and knee kp 150, kd 5, armature 0.03 kg·m², friction 0.3 N·m; ankles 40, 2, 0.02, 0.2.
  • Delay: motor commands arrive 0, 5 or 10 ms late (DelayedDCMotor), sensor observations 0 or 20 ms late, sampled per episode.
  • Noise: gyro ±0.2 rad/s, gravity vector ±0.05, joint angles ±0.01 rad, joint speeds ±0.5 rad/s; the height map gets drift, holes and 10 Hz updates.
  • Randomisation: ground friction 0.4–1.0 (static), torso mass ±15% and centre of mass ±3 cm, pushes to 0.5 m/s, random upper-body motion. Joint armature, friction, gains and motor strength are not randomised.
  • Asymmetric critic: the critic sees the true velocity and terrain; the actor only real sensors (page 16).
  • Sim-to-sim: every policy is exported to ONNX and replayed in MuJoCo (sim/mujoco/check_policy.py) on the current URDF, a different physics engine than training (Newton).

The step test uses the motor's own position mode (operation control with kp and kd, robstride.py type 1 frames), which is how the robot will run, so it identifies the motor and its built-in controller together. The playground uses pure torque commands instead because the maths stays linear; the driver supports that too (the thermal test sends kp = kd = 0 with a feed-forward torque). One caution: the torque in RobStride's feedback frame is computed by the driver from current, so checking Kt needs an external reference such as a load cell or a known weight on a lever.[15]

Open items that are sim-to-real questions

  • Roadmap item 2: output-bearing type, backlash and moment ratings.
  • Item 3: bus current and regen behaviour.
  • Item 4 and 13: real CAN timing with 3–5 motors per bus on the Orin, and whether a dual-channel adapter carries two 500 Hz buses.
  • Item 11: knee and hip-roll heating on slow stairs.
  • Stair robustness: the stair policy (rough v9, trained on the earlier 1.29 m body) falls in 1 of 15 runs on 15–20 cm stairs in simulation on the 1.44 m model, and a retrain on the new body did worse; the real robot will not do better than simulation (page 16).

The bench also chose its own scope: one RS04 and one RS03 are bought singly, and the remaining RS03 are ordered only after the bench passes (docs/decisions.md, 2026-10-26), so the motor models are known before most of the money is spent. (An earlier decision named an RS06 as the second bench unit; it was later replaced by the RS03 when the ankles went back to RS03.)

Where sources and the repo disagree
  • The README and decision log describe the delays as ranges (0–20 ms, 0–10 ms); the code samples discrete values (0 or 20 ms; 0, 5 or 10 ms). Published practice, for example Tan et al., models the measured latency itself[6]; the bench latency test will say which values matter.
  • Many published humanoid recipes randomise joint friction, armature or motor strength as well as contact and mass[13]; Marvin Bot does not yet, by choice, until the bench gives it a centre to randomise around.

Decisions you can make here

  • Which tests the bench must include to identify each parameter (a chirp for inertia, steady speeds for friction, a locked-output load cell for Kt).
  • Whether the fitted motor model is good enough (residual near the noise floor) or an actuator network is needed.
  • How wide to randomise each motor parameter around the fit: the fit's scatter over repeated tests and over temperature is a reasonable minimum. Wider ranges make the policy more robust but more cautious (the worst-corner error in the mini playground grows).
  • Which delay range to train with once the latency test reports median and p99 round trips.
  • How stiff the deploy gains should be given the measured delay: stiffer joints track better when the delay is small, but a delay hurts a stiff joint more (Try this, step 3).
