Reinforcement learning for walking

Nobody writes Marvin Bot's walking controller by hand. A neural network learns it by trial and error in thousands of simulated copies of the robot, steered only by a score called the reward. This page explains how that works, what the robot sees and does, and why getting the reward right is most of the job.

~35 minBefore this: 02 Balance, 14 Simulation, 15 PD control
You will learn
  • The vocabulary of reinforcement learning (agent, environment, policy, observation, action, reward, return, discount) and how PPO improves a policy safely.
  • Exactly what Marvin Bot's walking policy observes, outputs and is rewarded for, with the real term names and weights from the repo.
  • Why a reward is a specification that an optimiser will exploit (reward hacking), and how the project fixed a crouch, a waddle and toe-down swings by adding terms.
  • How curricula and domain randomisation make a simulated policy survive stairs, pushes and delays.

The idea in one picture

Think of training a dog with treats, except the dog is a neural network, the treats are numbers, and you can run 4,096 dogs at once inside a physics simulator. Every 20 ms (50 times a second) each simulated robot reads its sensors, the network outputs 12 numbers that move the leg joints, the simulator advances, and a reward function scores what happened: did it walk at the commanded speed, stay upright, keep its feet level, avoid overheating its knees? After a few seconds of experience from every robot, an algorithm nudges the network's weights so that actions which led to more reward than expected become more likely. Repeat a few thousand times and a walking gait emerges.[1][6]

What is a neural network? For this page, think of it as a big formula with a lot of adjustable knobs, called weights. Numbers go in (here: the robot's sensor readings), get multiplied by the weights, added up and passed through simple bends, layer after layer, and numbers come out (here: 12 joint targets). With random weights the output is nonsense; "training" means turning the knobs, a tiny bit at a time, so the output gets better. Marvin Bot's walking network is an MLP (multi-layer perceptron, the plainest kind): three layers of 256, 128 and 128 units, tens of thousands of weights in all, and ELU is the name of the "bend" used between layers. Nothing about walking is written into it; everything comes from the reward.

How to read this picture: The policy (blue) sends actions to the simulated robots (orange), which send back what the robot now senses and a reward score (green). Every 24 steps, the PPO box uses the collected experience to adjust the policy's weights (dashed arrow).

The catch: the network does not learn what you meant, it learns whatever earns the most reward. If crouching is stable and nothing penalises it, it crouches. The playground below lets you be the reward designer.

From first principles

The words

  • Agent: the thing that decides; here the policy network. Environment: everything else, the simulated robot, ground and stairs.
  • Step t: one tick of the loop, here every 0.02 s. The little t under a symbol just says "at step t".
  • Observation ot: the numbers the agent sees at step t (not the full state of the world: the robot cannot measure its own velocity directly, for example).
  • Action at: what the agent outputs, 12 numbers for the 12 leg joints.
  • Policy πθ(a|o): the network with weights θ. During training it outputs a mean and a spread (a Gaussian), so it tries slightly different actions each time; that randomness is how it explores.
  • Reward rt: one number per step saying how good that step was, the sum of many weighted terms.
  • Return Gt: the reward from now on, with future rewards counted less by the discount γ (between 0 and 1).[1]
Gt = rt + γ rt+1 + γ² rt+2 + … = Σk≥0 γk rt+k

With γ = 0.99 and a step every 0.02 s, a reward 100 steps (2 s) ahead counts 0.99100 ≈ 0.37 as much as one now. So the policy plans about two seconds ahead, enough for three or four footsteps. The goal of RL is to find θ that maximises the expected return.

Policy gradient: make good actions more likely

The basic recipe: run the policy, then for each action ask "was this better or worse than I expected from this situation?" That difference is the advantage Ât. Push up the probability of actions with positive advantage, push down the others. In symbols (∇θ, the gradient, means "which way to turn each knob θ to increase this", and the log is a mathematical convenience):[1][4]

∇θJ ≈ mean over samples of ∇θ log πθ(at|ot) · Ât

"Expected" comes from a second network, the value function or critic V(s), trained to predict the return from each state. This is an on-policy method: the data must come from the current policy, so each batch is used for a few updates and then thrown away. That is why massive parallel simulation matters so much: 4,096 robots × 24 steps = 98,304 fresh samples in about 2 s of GPU time.[6]

GAE: a low-noise advantage

Generalised Advantage Estimation[3] builds the advantage from one-step surprises, the temporal-difference errors δ, and blends them with weight (γλ)k:

δt = rt + γ V(st+1) − V(st) Ât = Σk≥0 (γλ)k δt+k

λ = 0 trusts the critic completely (low noise, but biased if the critic is wrong); λ = 1 adds up real rewards (unbiased, noisy). Marvin Bot uses λ = 0.95, so the advantage looks about 1/(1−0.99×0.95) ≈ 17 steps (0.34 s) ahead before leaning on the critic.

PPO: change the policy, but not too much

Plain policy gradients can take a step so large that the policy collapses and never recovers. Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)[2] keeps each update close to the data-collecting policy with the clipped surrogate objective, where rt(θ) = πθ(at|ot) / πold(at|ot):

LCLIP(θ) = mean[ min( rt(θ) Ât , clip(rt(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε) Ât ) ]

Taking the minimum makes it a pessimistic bound: you get credit for making a good action up to 20% more likely (ε = 0.2), no more, but you always pay in full for making a bad action more likely. The full loss adds a value-function error term (weight 1.0 in Marvin Bot's config) and a small entropy bonus (0.008) that keeps the policy exploring. rsl_rl also adapts the learning rate to hold the measured policy change (the KL divergence) near 0.01 per update.[9]

Mini playground: PPO's clipped objective and the discount

Now that the words are defined, see the two knobs at work. PPO compares the new policy with the one that collected the data through the probability ratio r = πnew(a|s) / πold(a|s). The clipped objective stops rewarding changes once r leaves [1−ε, 1+ε] in the direction the advantage pushes.[2]

Why learn walking at all?

A legged robot is underactuated (nothing bolts the pelvis to the world), contacts switch on and off, and the motors saturate. Classical controllers handle this with simplified models (see 02 Balance) and a lot of tuning. Learned policies have, since about 2019, outperformed hand-built controllers in robustness on rough ground: Hwangbo et al. trained a quadruped policy in simulation that transferred to hardware, helped by a learned actuator model[5]; Lee et al. extended it to challenging terrain with a teacher-student scheme[11]; Rudin et al. showed that thousands of GPU-parallel robots with a game-inspired terrain curriculum learn to walk in minutes[6], the recipe behind Isaac Lab's locomotion tasks and rsl_rl[8][9]. Radosavovic et al. carried it to a full-size humanoid outdoors with a transformer policy trained in simulation[7], and open frameworks such as Humanoid-Gym[15] and mjlab[14] package humanoid recipes.

Projected gravity deserves a word: if g = (0, 0, −1) in the world, rotating it into the pelvis frame gives a vector whose sideways component is −sin(roll)·cos(pitch). A level pelvis reads (0, 0, −1); it is how the policy "feels" tilt.

Actions: offsets around the stance, through a PD loop

The policy does not output torques. It outputs one number per leg joint, which becomes a position target around the default stance, and the joint's PD controller (see 15 PD control) turns that into torque at 200 Hz:

qtarget = qstance + 0.5 · a τ = kp (qtarget − q) − kd q̇, clipped to the motor's torque-speed limits

With the hip and knee gains (kp = 150 N·m/rad, kd = 5 N·m·s/rad), an action of 0.2 shifts the target by 0.1 rad (5.7°) and asks for 15 N·m before the joint moves. The action scale (0.5 rad per unit) is a design choice: at 0.25 the robot "never found a stepping gait" (README, flat v2). Small actions near zero mean "stand in the stance", which gives the random initial policy a sensible starting point.

Rewards: a weighted sum

Each term is a function of the state, multiplied by its weight; Isaac Lab also multiplies by the step time (0.02 s), so the weights read as "reward per second".[8] Tracking terms are bell curves of the error, for example

track_lin_vel_xy_exp = exp( −|vcmd − v|² / 0.25² ) (1.0 at perfect tracking, 0.37 at 0.25 m/s error)

Penalties are squared or clamped errors with negative weights. The full list is in "How Marvin Bot does it" below; the sandbox uses the main ones.

Reward hacking

The optimiser has no idea what "walking nicely" means: it maximises the number. Any behaviour that scores well but is not what you intended will be found, a problem known as reward hacking or specification gaming.[12] On a walking robot it shows up as crouching, shuffling, waddling, vibrating feet, or standing still. The fix is iterative: train, watch, measure in an independent simulator, add or reweight a term, resume. Marvin Bot's README is a record of exactly that loop.

Curricula and domain randomisation

A curriculum starts easy and gets harder as the policy improves: Rudin et al.'s terrain curriculum promotes robots that walk far on their tile to harder tiles and demotes the ones that fail.[6] Domain randomisation varies the physics (friction, masses, pushes, delays) from robot to robot so the policy cannot rely on any one value, which is what lets it survive the real robot's unknown values.[16][17] That topic continues on 20 Sim-to-real.

Asymmetric actor-critic

The critic is only used in training, so it may see things the real robot cannot: the true pelvis velocity, the exact terrain. A better-informed critic gives better advantages, while the actor stays limited to real sensors. This is the asymmetric actor-critic idea[10], a lighter cousin of the privileged teacher-student training of Lee et al.[11]

How Marvin Bot does it

In the repo Tasks MarvinBot-Velocity-Flat and MarvinBot-Velocity-Rough in sim/isaaclab/marvin_bot/tasks/velocity_env_cfg.py (after Isaac Lab's humanoid velocity tasks), custom terms in tasks/rewards.py, PPO settings in tasks/agents.py, terrain in tasks/terrains.py, curricula in tasks/curriculums.py, motors and delays in robot_cfg.py. Results and history in sim/isaaclab/README.md; reasoning in docs/decisions.md.

The set-up

  • Decoupled whole-body control: the policy drives only the 12 leg joints at 50 Hz. The waist and arm targets jump to random poses within half their range every 1–3 s, so the legs learn to balance whatever the upper body does.
  • Commands: forward −0.3 m/s up to a limit that starts at 0.5 and grows by 0.05 m/s to 1.2 m/s whenever the robots earn over 75% of the tracking reward; sideways ±0.3 m/s; turning ±1.0 rad/s; 20% of robots are told to stand still.
  • Episodes: 20 s; a fall is the pelvis below 0.35 m or tilted more than 1 rad.
  • PPO (rsl_rl): 24 steps per robot per iteration, 5 epochs over 4 mini-batches, clip 0.2, entropy 0.008, learning rate 1e-3 adapted to KL 0.01, γ 0.99, λ 0.95, gradient-norm clip 1.0; actor and critic MLPs 256-128-128 (flat) or 512-256-128 (rough), ELU activations, observation normalisation, and left-right mirror data augmentation (without it, earlier policies limped).[13]
  • Asymmetric critic: the critic also gets the base linear velocity and the exact height map; the actor gets noisy, delayed sensors and the mapped height scan.
  • Training cost: about 2.2–2.3 s per iteration with 4,096 robots on one workstation GPU, ~1.3 h per 2,000 iterations; flat v8 accumulated about 8,000 iterations (flat v7's 6,000 plus 2,000 on the 1.44 m body) and rough v9 about 14,000, each run resuming the last after a reward change.

Curricula

The rough terrain is a 10 × 20 grid of 8 m tiles, 10 difficulty rows: 45% stairs (4–20 cm risers in the code, 5–20 cm in the README; 28 cm treads, up and down), another 10% steep stairs (14–20 cm) on every row so hard stairs appear from the start, 10% boxes up to 10 cm, 15% rough ground to 5 cm, 10% slopes to 0.4 rad, 10% flat. Robots start on rows 0–2. Isaac Lab's default promotion (walk half a tile) failed at slow commanded speeds, so terrain_levels_tracking promotes a robot that survived, earned over 50% of the tracking reward and ended over 2 m from its spawn point, and demotes one that fell or earned under 40%. Rough v9 sits at terrain level 5.9 of 10.

Domain randomisation

  • Ground friction: static 0.4–1.0, dynamic 0.4–0.8.
  • Torso payload: mass × 1/1.15 to 1.15 (log-uniform), centre of mass ±3 cm forward and sideways, ±2 cm up.
  • Pushes: a sudden velocity change of up to ±0.5 m/s every 5–10 s; random start pose, velocity (±0.2) and joint angles (0.9–1.1 × stance).
  • Delays per episode: sensor observations 0 or 1 policy step (0 or 20 ms), motor commands 0, 1 or 2 physics steps (0, 5 or 10 ms) in DelayedDCMotor.
  • Motors: a DC-motor model (torque saturates at peak and falls linearly to zero at no-load speed); gains, armature and joint friction are fixed placeholders until the phase 2 bench.

Where the policies stand

Flat ground: flat v8, flat v7 plus 2,000 iterations on the current 1.44 m, 40.35 kg model, passes the MuJoCo cross-check: walk 0.53 m/s for 0.5 commanded, turn 0.83 rad/s for 0.8, fast walk 0.95 for 1.0 m/s, symmetric within 2%, with the knees bent about 38° on average through the stride (15° standing) and the hottest joint a knee at 68% of rated in the fast walk (sim/isaaclab/README.md).

Stairs: still rough v9, trained on the earlier 1.29 m body. In the cross-check on the 1.44 m model it passes 14 of 15 stair runs (15, 18 and 20 cm risers × five noise seeds; one fall on 15 cm). On its own, older body it failed 2 of 15. A retrain on the new body, rough v10 (3,000 more iterations), did worse, 6 of 15, and is not used; the stair policy is the one still to be retrained (docs/roadmap.md).

Where sources and the repo differ
  • Rudin et al.'s terrain curriculum promotes robots that walk far enough on their tile[6]; Marvin Bot found that too few robots got far at slow speeds and added tracking and a 2 m minimum distance (tasks/curriculums.py).
  • The PPO paper's reference settings use a fixed or annealed learning rate[2]; rsl_rl adapts it to a KL target (0.01), a common locomotion practice not in the original paper.
  • The README describes the delays as "0–20 ms" and "0–10 ms"; in the code they are discrete: 0 or 20 ms on observations and 0, 5 or 10 ms on commands, sampled per episode. Nothing between is simulated.

Decisions you can make here

  • Which behaviours your rewards must rule out explicitly (posture, roll, step width, swing foot tilt) and how strongly: check that the "good" gait wins with a margin, not by a hair. Every extra penalty closes a loophole but also makes the robot more cautious; pile on too many and standing still wins (try Over-penalised).
  • How much air-time reward to pay, and which formula: biped single-support time does not pay flight. More air time gives clear, lifted steps; too much (2.0) gave the waddle.
  • Whether rough v9's 1-in-15 stair failure rate on the 1.44 m body is acceptable for phase 3, or worth a better retrain (v10 was worse), more steep-stair tiles, history in the policy, or a teacher-student stage. Each option costs hours to days of GPU time, and none is guaranteed to beat v9.
  • PPO settings: clip range, γ (planning horizon) and λ (bias versus noise). A larger γ lets the robot plan further ahead but makes learning noisier and slower; the defaults (0.2, 0.99, 0.95) are the well-trodden choice.
  • Whether to retrain from scratch with the final reward set, to check the results do not depend on the resume history (flat v8 and rough v10 both resumed earlier runs). Several GPU-hours per policy (flat v8 took about 8,000 iterations), for confidence that the result can be reproduced.
