A walking humanoid carries its whole power station on its back. This page builds Marvin Bot's 48 V battery from single cells, estimates how long it lasts, follows the current from the pack through fuse, precharge and contactor to the motors, and shows why energy flowing *back* from braking joints can push the bus towards the actuators' 60 V limit.

## The idea in one picture

A robot that walks around cannot trail a power cable, so it carries a battery, like a cordless drill or an electric scooter. That battery must hold enough energy for a useful session (Marvin Bot's requirement is at least an hour), deliver big bursts of current when the legs push hard, and be wired so that a fault or an emergency stop is always safe.

Three words do most of the work. Think of water in a pipe: **voltage** (V, volts) is the pressure, **current** (A, amps) is the flow, and **power** (W, watts) is pressure × flow. **Energy** (Wh, watt-hours) is power kept up for a time: a 600 Wh battery can supply 600 W for one hour, or 300 W for two.

A lithium-ion cell is a small chemical energy store whose voltage depends on how full it is: about 4.2 V when full and about 3.0 V when empty, averaging 3.6 V (its "nominal" voltage).<sup class="cite">[\[1\]](#s1)</sup> One cell is far too low a voltage for 48 V motors, so cells are wired in **series** (voltages add): 13 in series (13S) give 13 × 3.6 = 46.8 V nominal and 13 × 4.2 = 54.6 V full. Each series position can hold several cells in **parallel** (capacities and current limits add): 3 in parallel (3P) of 4.5 Ah cells give 13.5 Ah. The energy is volts times amp-hours:

<div class="eq">

E = (S × V<sub>cell</sub>) × (P × Q<sub>cell</sub>) = 46.8 V × 13.5 Ah ≈ 632 Wh (13S3P of 3.6 V, 4.5 Ah cells)<br /> runtime ≈ usable energy / average power

</div>

The rest of the page is about the two numbers that are hard: how much of the energy you may use, and what the average power is.

## From first principles

### Cells, packs and the words for them

- **Capacity** (Ah, amp-hours): the charge a cell delivers from full to empty. 4.5 Ah means 4.5 A for an hour, or 45 A for six minutes.
- **Energy** (Wh, watt-hours) = average voltage × capacity. Molicel lists the P45B at 3.6 V, 4.5 Ah, 16.2 Wh.<sup class="cite">[\[1\]](#s1)</sup> 39 cells × 16.2 Wh = 632 Wh.
- <b><a class="g" href="../glossary/#c-rate">C-rate</a></b>: current divided by capacity. 1C empties a cell in an hour; 10C in six minutes. The P45B is rated for 45 A continuous, 10C.<sup class="cite">[\[1\]](#s1)[\[3\]](#s3)</sup> In a 3P pack the cells share the current, so the pack can supply 135 A.
- <b><a class="g" href="../glossary/#state-of-charge">State of charge</a> (SoC)</b>: how full the pack is, 0-100%. <b><a class="g" href="../glossary/#depth-of-discharge">Depth of discharge</a></b> is the share you actually use.
- <b><a class="g" href="../glossary/#internal-resistance">Internal resistance</a></b>: every cell behaves like an ideal voltage source with a small resistor in series. Molicel gives at most 13.8 mΩ for the P45B.<sup class="cite">[\[1\]](#s1)</sup> A 13S3P pack is about 13 × 13.8 / 3 ≈ 60 mΩ before wiring: at 80 A the pack voltage sags by about 5 V.

<b>Why 21700 high-discharge cells?</b> "21700" is the size (21 mm diameter, 70 mm long). High-discharge cells such as the P45B trade a little energy density for low internal resistance, so they can deliver the short bursts of current a robot's legs draw without overheating.

<b>Why charge only to 4.1 V?</b> A lithium-ion cell ages faster at high voltage. Battery University's table gives 300-500 cycles when charged to 4.20 V and 600-1,000 cycles at 4.06 V, with 81% of the energy; 4.13 V gives 400-700 cycles and 90%.<sup class="cite">[\[2\]](#s2)</sup> Marvin Bot's 4.1 V rule sits between those rows (about 86% of the energy, interpolated). It also leaves 1.3 V more headroom below the actuators' 60 V for regenerative braking.

### Where the power goes

The playground adds up four terms for each activity (standing, walking, stairs):

<div class="small">

In the formulas below, η (eta) is an efficiency between 0 and 1; I<sub>ph</sub> and R<sub>ph</sub> are the current and resistance of one motor phase (winding), and R<sub>LL</sub> is the resistance measured between two of the motor's three wires.

</div>

<div class="eq">

P = P<sub>computer</sub> / η<sub>DC-DC</sub> + P<sub>electronics</sub> + Σ P<sub>Cu</sub> + P<sub>mech</sub> / η<sub>gear</sub>, then × 1.10 for wiring, converter and driver losses<br /> P<sub>Cu</sub> = 3 I<sub>ph</sub>² R<sub>ph</sub> = 1.5 (τ / K<sub>t</sub>)² R<sub>LL</sub> (copper loss of one motor at output torque τ)

</div>

- **Computer**: the Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB has power modes of 15, 30 (default) and 50 W, plus an unconstrained MAXN mode<sup class="cite">[\[4\]](#s4)</sup>; the playground uses 60 W for MAXN, the figure NVIDIA's tools quote for the 64 GB module, and divides by a 90% DC-DC efficiency.
- **Electronics** (estimate, 30 W): 23 RobStride drivers idling at about 1 W each, the Feetech servos' 12 V rail, the depth camera, IMU and CAN adapters. These are guesses until measured.
- **Copper loss**: a motor's torque comes from current, and current through winding resistance makes heat. Torque τ needs current τ/K<sub>t</sub> (K<sub>t</sub> = <a class="g" href="../glossary/#torque-constant">torque constant</a>, newton-metres of torque per amp), and heat goes with current squared. The RS04's torque constant is 2.1 N·m/A and resellers list its winding resistance as 0.16 Ω line to line<sup class="cite">[\[5\]](#s5)[\[6\]](#s6)</sup>: at its 40 N·m rating that is 1.5 × (40/2.1)² × 0.16 ≈ 87 W of heat. For the RS03, RS06 and RS00, without published resistances, the playground scales that figure by motor mass (55, 38 and 19 W at rating; an estimate).<br />Because loss goes with τ², the average heat depends on the *root-mean-square* (RMS) torque: half the rated torque RMS costs a quarter of the rated loss.
- **Mechanical work**: positive work done on the body. For walking the playground uses a mechanical cost of transport (how much work it takes to move each newton of weight one metre) of 0.1 (work per unit weight per metre; an estimate) at 0.5 m/s: 0.1 × 41 kg × 9.81 × 0.5 ≈ 20 W. For stairs, lifting 41 kg up an 18 cm step every 2 s: 41 × 9.81 × 0.09 m/s ≈ 36 W. Both are divided by 85% gearbox efficiency. Negative work (lowering the body) is assumed lost as heat, not recovered.

The surprise for most newcomers is that **heat, not lifting, dominates**: holding a bent knee costs copper loss even though nothing moves. That is why the stair torques in [05 Leg loads and stairs](../05-leg-loads-stairs/) matter for runtime as well as for motor temperature.

### Peak current

Average power sets runtime; **peak** current sets the cell, fuse and wire choices. The playground takes one stance leg on a stair (3 RS04 at hip pitch, hip roll, knee and 3 RS03 at hip yaw and ankle) at a chosen share of peak torque and 4 rad/s, adds copper loss and mechanical power, and divides by the pack voltage near empty (3.5 V/cell). With the repo's "peaks ≤ 70% of peak rating" rule this gives about 80 A for a fraction of a second, within the 135 A the three parallel cells allow and just under the 100 A main fuse.

A fuse at a modest overload takes from seconds to minutes to blow (its time-current curve), so sub-second peaks at this level pass, but the margin is thin and the phase 2 bench test must confirm the real peaks. `electrical/harness.yaml` estimates 78.5 A continuous for the main path with a different method (rated torque at half the no-load speed, half the motors at once), so both point to the same order of current.

## Playground 2: regenerative braking and the 60 V margin

A bicycle dynamo makes electricity when the wheel turns; an electric motor does the same when something turns it. So when a motor slows a moving limb, it works as a generator: the limb's kinetic energy turns into current that flows *back* onto the 48 V bus. If the battery can absorb it, the bus voltage rises only by current × internal resistance. If it cannot (the BMS has blocked charging because the pack is full, the contactor is open, or the robot runs from a bench supply that cannot sink current), the energy has nowhere to go but the capacitors on the bus, and their voltage climbs fast:

Why it matters: the RobStride drivers are only rated to 60 V. A full pack is already at 54.6 V, so there is little room for the voltage to climb before parts are damaged.

<div class="eq">

kinetic energy of a swinging limb: E = ½ J ω² (J = moment of inertia, kg·m²; ω = joint speed, rad/s)<br /> battery connected: V = V<sub>OC</sub> + I R<sub>pack</sub>, I = P<sub>regen</sub> / V<br /> battery blocked: ½ C V<sub>peak</sub>² = ½ C V<sub>0</sub>² + η E → V<sub>peak</sub> = √(V<sub>0</sub>² + 2ηE / C)

</div>

<div class="small">

In words: V<sub>OC</sub> is the pack's resting voltage, R<sub>pack</sub> its internal resistance, P<sub>regen</sub> the power flowing back; C is the total capacitance on the bus (capacitors are small, fast energy stores, measured in farads; a millifarad, mF, is a thousandth), V<sub>0</sub> the voltage before braking and η the share of the limb's energy that reaches the bus. A **clamp** is a circuit that switches a resistor across the bus above a set voltage (a "brake chopper"), or a TVS diode that conducts above it, turning excess energy into heat.

</div>

<RawHtml html={widget2} />

:::note[Try this]
1. On **Marvin Bot today** the pack swallows a leg stop with a rise of well under 1 V. Now press **Hard stop, four limbs**: all four limbs stopped from 10 rad/s as hard as the motors' peak torque allows (a fall, or a policy glitch) push about 60 A back, and with a full 4.2 V pack the bus reaches about 59 V, a whisker under the limit, before counting wiring inductance.
2. Press **Full pack, BMS blocks charging**. About 20 J into a few millifarads sends the bus far beyond 60 V. This is the case the clamp exists for, and why the repo charges to 4.1 V, not 4.2 V.
3. Press **Bench supply, no clamp**, then <b>+ 57 V clamp</b>. Most bench supplies cannot absorb current at all; the clamp burns the excess as heat (the "energy into clamp" readout).
4. Double the bus capacitance. The peak falls, but only as √C: capacitors alone are an expensive way to absorb regen.
:::

A second, less obvious path: a motor spun faster than its no-load speed generates a <a class="g" href="../glossary/#back-emf">back-EMF</a> (the voltage a spinning motor makes) above the bus voltage and pushes current through its driver's transistor diodes even when the driver is disabled. A robot falling with the contactor open can therefore still pump energy into the (now isolated) motor-side capacitors. The clamp belongs on the motor side of the contactor for this reason.

### Precharge, in numbers

Every motor driver and DC-DC converter has capacitors across its input. When the <a class="g" href="../glossary/#contactor">contactor</a> (a heavy-duty, electrically operated switch) closes onto empty capacitors, the only thing limiting the current is the resistance of the loop: 54 V across 0.1 Ω is over 500 A for a fraction of a millisecond, enough to weld contactor contacts or blow the fuse. A precharge resistor first fills the capacitors slowly: with R = 47 Ω and C = 5 mF the time constant RC is 0.24 s, the peak current about 1.2 A, and after 5RC (1.2 s) the capacitors are within 1% of the pack voltage, so the contactor closes with almost no current. (RC, resistance × capacitance, is how long the charging takes: after one RC the capacitors are 63% full.) The resistor briefly absorbs ½CV² ≈ 7 J. (The capacitance is an estimate; the real value sets the resistor and the precharge time.)

## How Marvin Bot does it

:::tip[In the repo]
(`docs/decisions.md`, 2026-10-25, "power: 13S3P quick-release pack, keep-alive supply, 4.1 V charging")

- <b>Pack:</b> 13S3P of 21700 high-discharge cells, \~630 Wh, \~3.4 kg: about 1.5× the runtime of 13S2P for \~1.1 kg more. <i>Why:</i> comfortable margin over the 1 h requirement and over the stair current peaks.
- **Quick-release cradle** with an anti-spark blind-mate connector (AS150 class: a plug that makes contact without sparking and lines itself up). <i>Why:</i> swap packs in seconds.
- **Keep-alive battery** for the computer through an <a class="g" href="../glossary/#ideal-diode-or">ideal-diode OR</a> (a circuit that takes power from whichever source is higher, with almost no loss); the computer is fed upstream of the contactor. <i>Why:</i> an e-stop or pack swap never reboots the computer or loses its logs.
- **Charge to 4.1 V/cell** (53.3 V) daily; regen clamp at about 56-58 V; DC-DCs rated for 75 V input. <i>Why:</i> longer cell life and room below the 60 V limit.
- **Fuses and wires** (`docs/design/assembly_wiring.md`, `electrical/harness.yaml`; current expected / fuse / wire gauge, where AWG is the American wire size, smaller number = thicker wire): main 78.5 A / 100 A / 6 AWG; trunk 51.3 A / 80 A / 8 AWG; each leg 24.3 A / 40 A; each arm 13.6 A / 20 A. Positions in the torso: `cad/electrical.py`.
- <b>Parts and prices:</b> BOM power group, $1,633 (39 Molicel P45B cells at $9.95, a 13S 150 A BMS with CAN at $145, charger, Class-T fuse, contactor, precharge, DC-DCs, ideal-diode controller, keep-alive pack, regen clamp).
:::

Requirements: R30 runtime ≥ 1 h of mixed walking and standing; R31 quick-release pack with the computer staying up during a swap; R32 pack voltage plus regen spikes below the actuators' 60 V limit (`docs/requirements.md`). The BMS reports over the seventh CAN bus at 10 Hz ([10 CAN buses](../10-can-bus/)), and the robot sits down and cuts actuator power at about 15% (`docs/safety.md`).

## Trade-offs and alternatives

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| <b>13S3P, 4.1 V daily (chosen)</b> | \~1.5 h (repo), \~135 A of cell current, 1.3 V more regen headroom | \~3.4 kg, high in the torso |
| 13S2P | \~1.1 kg lighter (the repo lists it as a mass reserve) | runtime at the 1 h limit; 90 A cell limit near the stair peaks |
| Charge to 4.2 V every day | \~15% more energy | roughly half the cycle life; 54.6 V leaves 5.4 V to the 60 V limit |
| 12S pack (50.4 V full) | more regen margin | less voltage for motor speed; 12S BMS and chargers less common at this current |
| Two packs OR-ed (v2 in the repo) | swap one pack while walking | more mass, more electronics |
| Computer after the contactor | simpler wiring | every e-stop reboots the computer and loses logs |

:::caution[Where sources and the repo disagree]
- Runtime: the repo states \~1.5 h (13S3P) and \~1 h (13S2P) without a published power model. This page's first-principles model with its default assumptions gives about 1.6 h and 1.1 h at 4.1 V and a 15% reserve. The difference is well inside the uncertainty of the per-mode torque and electronics estimates.
- Voltage limit: `description/actuators.yaml` notes RobStride's manual gives 24-60 V for the RS04 while its specification README says 15-60 V; the upper 60 V limit, which matters here, agrees.
:::

## Decisions you can make here

- <b>Pack size (S and P).</b> Trade: 13S2P saves about 1.1 kg high in the torso but drops runtime to about 1.1 h and puts the stair peaks (about 82 A) close to the 90 A the cells allow.
- <b>Charge voltage.</b> Trade: 4.2 V gives about 20% more runtime, but roughly halves cycle life and leaves only 5.4 V of headroom to 60 V for braking energy.
- **The reserve at which the robot sits down** (15% today). Trade: a lower reserve adds runtime, but leaves less margin for the voltage sag on a hard push near empty and ages the cells faster.
- <b>Clamp voltage and position, and bus capacitance.</b> Trade: a lower clamp protects the drivers better but wastes more energy as heat and may trigger in normal braking; capacitors only help as √C and add bulk.
- <b>Orin power mode.</b> Trade: 15 W instead of 30 W adds about 0.1 h with the default day, at the cost of slower perception and planning on the robot.
